# How wording and missing context shape a reader's response

Research for ticket #154 on the map "Write to agents with theory of mind" (#146), 2026-09-30.

People answer the request they read rather than the one the writer meant, treating every option, example, number and hint in it as a signal, and they rarely notice or ask when they have misread it. Language models show many of the same pulls, some more strongly and some that people don't have, and this note gives the evidence for each with how well it has held up.

## How to read this note

**What it covers.** Findings from survey methodology, social and cognitive psychology, psycholinguistics, forensic interviewing, radiology, design research, management, military doctrine, ethnomethodology, medicine, and language-model research. The question was what these fields know about two things: how the wording of a request shapes the response, and how to write for a reader whose context differs from the writer's.

**How it was done.** I read some sources myself and four reading lanes read the rest, each told to trace claims to the original work. Every entry says what was actually read:

- *original*: the paper, book or dissertation itself.
- *abstract*: only the original's abstract.
- *secondary*: someone else's account, named where it matters. Treat these numbers as unconfirmed.

Where a number came from a tool that summarizes web pages rather than from the page text, the entry says so. Check those before quoting them anywhere that matters.

**Labels.** Each finding carries two labels.

- *Standing* says how the field sees it now: **replicated** (repeated by others with the same result), **narrowed** (holds only under some conditions, or smaller than first reported), **contested** (serious published disagreement), **failed** (a large replication did not find it), or **single study** (not repeated that I found).
- *Population* says who was tested: **people**, **models**, or **both**. A finding about people is a hypothesis about models until someone tests it on models, and the reverse.

**Terms used throughout.**

- A *brief* is the message an agent writes to start another agent on a job. A *lane* is the agent that receives it. The *writer* and *reader* are whoever writes and reads a text, person or model.
- *Effect size d* is the difference between two groups in standard deviations. By convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
- *RLHF* (reinforcement learning from human feedback) is the training step that tunes a model toward answers people rate highly.

**A warning about this note's own shape.** The findings below are grouped by the mechanisms I saw in the evidence. That grouping is one reading, not a complete list of the ways writing goes wrong. Manuel's point about lists applies here too: a reader of this note who treats its section headings as the full space will miss what they don't name.

## The short version

1. **Readers assume everything in a request was put there on purpose.** Options, scale ranges, examples, the order of questions and what is left out are all read as signals of what the writer wants. The pull is strongest where the reader's own picture is weakest: vague terms, weak memories, hard questions. By that pattern a reader starting with little context is among the most steerable, though for models this is an inference, not a measurement. (Survey methodology, well replicated in people.)

2. **A list of options mostly bounds what comes back, even with an "other" slot.** In one experiment, four problems offered in a list went from 2% of answers to 60%. The same list also brought back answers people had forgotten. Examples copy themselves into the answer, and giving part of a list suppresses recall of the rest. (Replicated in people. Models show strong option and format effects too, but nobody has tested a non-exhaustive example list on an open-ended search task like finding code smells.)

3. **What the writer believes leaks into the answer.** Presuppositions plant memories. Interviewers who expect guilt ask guilt-presuming questions. Experts told the context of a case change their own earlier conclusions. In models, a user's stated opinion, a hint in the examples, or a line of PR framing moves the answer a lot. In one 2026 study, telling a model the code was bug-free cut vulnerability detection from 97% to 4% for a small model and from 96% to 89% for Claude Opus 4.5. Blunt attempts to lead can backfire. Subtle framing works better. (Replicated in people. Measured many times in models.)

4. **Rules about how to do a task change the work, often against the goal.** A reporting threshold makes reviewers withhold findings they already made. The instruction decides what gets seen: people counting passes miss a gorilla, and in one 2026 preprint a narrow task instruction stopped models reporting critical findings they reported otherwise. Specific goals help on simple tasks and hurt on complex, novel ones. Guidance that helps novices hurts experts. Controlling wording lowers creativity where the same limit stated as information does not. Doctrine and sociology agree that instructions are always incomplete and that the doer has to fill the gap in the situation. (Mixed: several replicated, some contested, one vendor observation for current Claude models.)

5. **Writers overestimate how well they are understood, and telling them to "consider the reader" barely helps.** Tappers expected 50% of listeners to name a tapped tune. 2.5% did. In one paper of 25 experiments, deliberately imagining the other person's view did not make people more accurate. What did help was real signal from the reader: feedback, doing the reader's task, transcripts of real readers, or asking. (The curse of knowledge is replicated in people. The perspective-taking result rests mainly on that one paper and two smaller ones. Models show the same pattern: they can say what another party knows but fail to use it, and a reminder of that state fixes much of the gap.)

6. **Meaning built in one exchange does not travel to people who weren't in it, even if they heard every word.** A partner who heard a whole conversation but took no part in it was treated by the speaker almost like a stranger. Two different people, or two different pairs, pick the same word for a thing in only 10 to 20% of cases. This is the human evidence for Manuel's complaint about terms defined in a context the reader never saw. (Replicated in people. The model evidence points the same way.)

7. **Readers who misunderstand rarely know it and rarely ask, even when invited.** Scripted survey interviews got atypical cases right 28% of the time, and interviews where the interviewer could clarify got 87%. Respondents asked for help on 4% of the occasions help was given. Current models almost never ask on underspecified coding tasks. When a request comes in pieces over several turns, performance drops 39% on average. (Replicated in people and in models.)

8. **People and models differ in ways that matter.** Models don't reproduce most human survey biases one for one. They shift under changes people ignore, such as formatting and typos. Sycophancy, meaning agreement with the user against the evidence, grows with some kinds of training. More capable models resist framing better but not fully. No study I found measures human and model suggestibility on one scale, so "models are ten times more suggestible" is untested either way.

## 1. Readers treat every part of a request as meant

**The mechanism.** Schwarz's account of survey answering applies Grice's cooperative principle: listeners assume a speaker says what is relevant, true, and as much as needed. Respondents extend that assumption to every feature of a question. Clark and Schober called it the "interpretability presumption". Options, scale values, the reference period, earlier questions and even the sponsor are read as clues to what the asker wants.

- Schwarz, N. (1996). *Cognition and Communication: Judgmental Biases, Research Methods, and the Logic of Conversation*. Erlbaum. Schwarz, N. (1999). Self-reports: How the questions shape the answers. *American Psychologist* 54(2):93–105, [doi:10.1037/0003-066X.54.2.93](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.2.93). The PDF is a scan with no extractable text. The same arguments were read in the authors' own reviews: Schwarz & Oyserman 2001, *American Journal of Evaluation* 22(2):127–160 ([PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/01_aje_schw_oys_behavior.pdf)), and Strack & Schwarz 2007 ([PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/12/24HcoyjCfjM1E.pdf)). *Standing:* the framework is broadly accepted. Some showcase demonstrations have failed (below). *Population:* people.

**Scale ranges change the answer.** German respondents reported daily TV viewing on one of two scales. With a low-range scale, 16.2% reported more than 2½ hours. With a high-range scale, 37.5% did. Schwarz, Hippler, Deutsch & Strack (1985), *Public Opinion Quarterly* 49(3):388–395 ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/49/3/388/1879582)). Secondary (Schwarz's own later reviews and Plous 1993). A related study read in the authors' review found the effect largest where the thing asked about was vague. For "responsiveness to weather" the gap was 75% against 21%. For "excessive perspiration" it was 50% against 42% (Schwarz & Scheuring 1992). *Standing:* replicated across health, sexual and consumer behavior. *Population:* people.

**Numbers on a scale change what the words mean.** The same question with the same end labels, scored 0 to 10 or −5 to +5: 34% chose 0–5 on the first, 13% chose the equivalent −5 to 0 on the second. Respondents read the numbers as telling them what "not at all successful" meant. Schwarz, Knäuper et al. (1991), *POQ* 55:570–582. Secondary (Schwarz & Oyserman 2001). *Population:* people.

**Being asked implies the thing exists.** About 30% of representative samples give opinions on issues that don't exist (Bishop et al. 1980, *POQ* 44:198–209, percentage unconfirmed). German students read a made-up "educational contribution" as a fee or a grant depending on the question before it (Strack, Schwarz & Wänke 1991). Secondary (Strack & Schwarz 2007). *Population:* people.

**A feature repeated everywhere stops carrying meaning.** When the same reference period was attached to many unrelated questions, it stopped shaping how people read them. Respondents treated it as boilerplate. A feature unique to one question carries meaning. A feature shared by all carries little. Igou, Bless & Schwarz (2002). Secondary (Strack & Schwarz 2007). *Standing:* single study as far as I found. *Population:* people. This bears on templates in both directions: fixed text in a template may be read past, and text that varies per job gets read as the point.

**Presuppositions work only when the asker seems cooperative.** Listeners used a leading presupposition when they assumed the asker was sincere. When the asker had a visible agenda, the presupposition largely stopped working. Dodd & Bradshaw (1980). Secondary (Strack & Schwarz 2007). *Population:* people. A model-side parallel is in section 3: blunt attempts to bias an AI code reviewer backfired, subtle ones worked.

**Question order.** In 1948, support for letting Communist reporters into the US was 36% when asked first and 73% after a question about American reporters in Russia (Hyman & Sheatsley 1950). Secondary (Schuman & Ludwig 1983, [ASA PDF](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/papers/1982_002.pdf)). *Standing:* replicated by Schuman & Presser in 1980 with a smaller effect, and across 14 samples in 12 countries (Stark et al. 2020, *Sociological Methods & Research* 49(3):567–602, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0049124117747304), abstract). *Population:* people.

A different order effect, the showcase for the conversational account, has not held. Strack, Martin & Schwarz (1988) found dating and life satisfaction correlated much more when the dating question came first. Schimmack & Oishi (2005), *JPSP* 89(3):395–406 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.3.395), abstract), failed to replicate it and found such effects weak in a meta-analysis. Many Labs 2 (Klein et al. 2018, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918810225), original) reran the sister study with N = 7,460: the original r = .67 against .32 became .38 against .44, reversed and small. *Standing:* failed. The general mechanism is not refuted, but this example of it should not be cited.

**Forbid versus allow.** In 1941, 62% said "no" to *allowing* speeches against democracy, while only 46% said "yes" to *forbidding* them (Rugg 1941, *POQ* 5(1):91–92). Many Labs 1 (Klein et al. 2014, *Social Psychology* 45(3):142–152, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-9335/a000178), original) replicated it across 36 samples at d = 1.96, larger than the original, and varying by site. Holleman (2006), *Quality & Quantity* 40:1–38 ([PDF](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/62915037/Holleman2006_Article_TheMeaningsOfYesAndNoAnExplana.pdf), original), found both wordings measure the same attitude. What differs is how people map it onto "yes" and "no". *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people. The figures quoted for Rugg differ between sources, probably because of how "don't know" answers are counted.

**Language models.** Tjuatja, Chen, Wu, Talwalkar & Neubig (2024), "Do LLMs Exhibit Human-like Response Biases? A Case Study in Survey Design", *TACL* 12:1011–1026 ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.56/), original) tested nine models (the Llama 2 family, Solar, GPT-3.5) on five survey biases, including forbid/allow and response order. No model matched human patterns across all five. RLHF-tuned chat models were less human-like than base models. All models shifted under changes that do not move people, such as typos and letter swaps. Dominguez-Olmedo, Hardt & Mendler-Dünner (2024, NeurIPS, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07951), abstract) found 43 models answering the US Census survey were driven by option order and labels, favoring "A". Rupprecht, Ahnert & Strohmaier (2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07188), abstract) found all nine models tested favored the *last* option. Which position wins depends on the setup. *Standing:* the fact of sensitivity is replicated. Its direction varies. *Population:* models. **Implication:** survey findings are hypotheses to test on models, not results to transfer.

## 2. Offered options and examples bound what comes back

**Open versus closed questions.** Schuman & Presser (1979), "The open and closed question", *American Sociological Review* 44(5):692–712 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/2094521)), asked US national samples what they would most prefer in a job, in open or closed form. In the closed form, all but 1% of answers stayed inside the five offered choices. In the open form, nearly 60% of answers fell outside them. A closed list rebuilt from the open answers still covered only 58% of what people said openly. A sex difference in the answers appeared only in the closed form. Secondary (Kalton & Schuman 1980, [ASA PDF](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/papers/1980_005.pdf), with Schuman as an author). *Standing:* replicated and textbook. *Population:* people.

Schuman and Presser did not conclude that open questions are better. An open question carries its own frame and leaves out what seems to go without saying, and a well-built closed question was sometimes more valid. Singer & Couper (2017, [doi](https://doi.org/10.12758/mda.2017.01)) argue for using more open questions.

**A list with an "other" slot still bounds the answer.** Schuman & Scott (1987), *Science* 236(4804):957–959 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.236.4804.957)). 178 people were asked the open question "What is the most important problem facing this country today?" 171 got a closed version listing four problems and explicitly inviting them to name a different one. The four listed problems took 2% of open answers and 60% of closed answers. The same paper shows the other side. "Invention of the computer" was named by 1–2% as the most important event of the past 50 years when asked openly, and chosen by about 30% when listed. A list can remind people of something they would have counted but didn't think of. Secondary, from Plous 1993, ch. 6 ([PDF](https://courses.eller.arizona.edu/mgmt/delaney/p_chapter6.pdf)), because the Science page refused access. *Standing:* direction replicated (Reja et al. 2003 found higher percentages in closed forms with similar rankings). *Population:* people. Krosnick & Presser (2010, *Handbook of Survey Research*, ch. 9, [PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2009/2009_handbook_krosnick.pdf), original) state that respondents keep to the offered choices even when "other" is available.

This is the closest human evidence for Manuel's "multiple choice question that you didn't realize you were asking".

**Examples narrow and steer what people recall.** Tourangeau, Conrad, Couper & Ye (2014), "The effects of providing examples in survey questions", *POQ* 78(1):100–125 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nft083), abstract). Examples of commonly eaten foods raised reported consumption. Atypical examples moved answers more than typical ones, because people think of typical items anyway. The authors' account: respondents judge from a small set of category members, and the examples change which ones and how many. A follow-up (Tourangeau, Sun, Conrad & Couper 2017, *IJPOR* 29(4):690–702, abstract) found answers copied how general or specific the examples were. *Standing:* little independent replication found. *Population:* people.

**Giving part of a list suppresses recall of the rest.** *Part-list cueing* is the finding that giving people some items from a list as cues makes them recall fewer of the remaining items. Slamecka (1968), *J. Exp. Psych.* 76(4):504–513 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025695), not read). Meta-analysis: Pepe, Moyer, Peña & Rajaram (2023), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 30:1243–1272 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02263-9), abstract): 109 samples, N = 5,605, a "robust, medium-sized" impairment. It does not depend much on the number of cues. Under some conditions cueing helps instead (Lehmer & Bäuml 2018). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people, tested with word lists.

**Design fixation.** Jansson & Smith (1991), *Design Studies* 12(1):3–11 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0142-694X(91)90003-F)). Engineering designers shown an example copied its features, including flawed ones, even when told the flaws. Secondary (Smith's later chapter), numbers unconfirmed. Smith, Ward & Schumacher (1993), *Memory & Cognition* 21(6):837–845 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03202751), secondary): people shown three examples included their features, and being told to make ideas very different did not reduce it. Chrysikou & Weisberg (2005, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.5.1134), abstract) found that naming the flawed elements to avoid did reduce fixation. The meta-analysis by Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan (2015), *Design Studies* 39:70–99 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.destud.2015.04.004), secondary), found examples produced more example-related ideas across fewer categories, but also more novel ones, and fewer and less common examples helped. Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016) found "great variety" in methods and results across 25 studies. *Standing:* contested. The effect is real but depends on the field, and on how many examples are shown and how unusual they are. *Population:* people.

**A known method blocks a better one.** The *Einstellung effect* is when a familiar way of solving a problem stops people seeing a better one. Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet (2008), "Why good thoughts block better ones", *Cognition* 108(3):652–661 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.05.005), author manuscript). Chess candidate masters given a position with a familiar five-move mate all found it. They reported that they had looked for something shorter, but eye tracking showed their gaze stayed on the familiar solution's squares. Players given a version with only the short solution found it. What people said they were doing did not match where they looked. *Standing:* small sample. The Einstellung effect itself goes back to Luchins (1942) and is long replicated. *Population:* people.

**When people can add to the list, the best answers often come from outside it.** Salganik & Levy (2015), "Wiki surveys: Open and quantifiable social data collection", *PLOS ONE* ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1202.0500), original). In two surveys where respondents voted on ideas and could add their own, 8 of the 10 highest-scoring ideas for New York City's PlaNYC and 7 of the top 10 for the OECD were contributed by respondents rather than seeded by the organizers. The authors attribute it to volume and variance. There were many more contributed ideas, with a wider spread of scores, although their average score was lower. *Standing:* two case studies, not an experiment. *Population:* people.

**Language models.**

- *Option letters and order.* Zheng, Zhou, Meng, Zhou & Huang (2024, ICLR, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03882), abstract): 20 models on 3 benchmarks prefer particular option letters because of a prior on the letter tokens. Pezeshkpour & Hruschka (2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11483), abstract): reordering options changed performance by 13% to 75%, most where the model was torn between two options. *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* models.
- *Closed formats change the content.* Röttger et al. (2024, ACL, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16786), abstract): models gave substantively different answers when not forced into survey options, and open answers differed again. This is the closest model evidence that a closed format changes what the model says, not just how.
- *Examples teach the shape of the answer.* Min et al. (2022, EMNLP, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837), abstract): across 12 models, random labels in examples barely hurt. The format, the set of possible labels and the kind of input mattered. *Standing:* narrowed. Large models do follow the labels (Wei et al. 2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03846)). Zhao et al. (2021, ICML, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09690), abstract): GPT-3 results swung with the choice and order of examples.
- *Anchoring on example code.* Jones & Steinhardt (2022), "Capturing Failures of Large Language Models via Human Cognitive Biases", NeurIPS ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12299), original). Codex given a related "anchor" function copied its lines into 32–61% of solutions. A framing line reappeared verbatim in 81% of outputs against 4.5% without it. *Standing:* 2022 Codex, not rerun on current models.
- *Models already converge.* Jiang et al. (2025), "Artificial Hivemind", NeurIPS best paper ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22954), full text via a summarizing tool): across 26,070 open-ended queries and 70+ models, 79% of cases had average self-similarity above 0.8, and different models' answers were 71–82% similar. An example adds its pull to a system that is already narrow.
- *People handed AI examples.* Doshi & Hauser (2024), *Science Advances* 10(28), eadn5290 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290), secondary). Writers given GPT-4 story ideas wrote more novel stories individually, but the stories became more similar to each other. *Population:* people.

**The gap.** Neither lane nor I found a controlled study that gives a model a non-exhaustive list of examples on an open-ended task (code smells, bug classes, review criteria) and measures what it then misses outside the list. The human evidence (Schuman & Scott, part-list cueing, design fixation) predicts a loss. The model evidence on options and formats is consistent with one. The experiment itself has not been run that I found.

## 3. What the writer believes leaks into the answer

This section covers what Manuel calls leading the witness: a question or brief carries the asker's belief, and the answer moves toward it.

**Presupposition.** Loftus (1975), "Leading questions and the eyewitness report", *Cognitive Psychology* 7(4):560–572 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(75)90023-7), original). After a film, one group was asked how fast a car was going when it passed "the barn". There was no barn. A week later, 17.3% of that group said they had seen a barn, against 2.7% of the others. In a further experiment, a false presupposition led 29.2% to say they had seen nonexistent items. Simply asking whether an item had been there gave 15.6%, against 8.4% for controls. Asking about a thing plants it. *Standing:* the misinformation effect is replicated over decades (Loftus 2005, *Learning & Memory* 12(4):361–366, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.94705), abstract). *Population:* people.

The more famous Loftus & Palmer (1974) verb effect is weaker than its reputation. "Smashed" gave a mean speed estimate of 40.8 mph and "contacted" 31.8 mph (secondary). Holmes, Kassin & Flusberg (2024, CogSci, [PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0wk430xs/qt0wk430xs_noSplash_9d113f3a391c35d2c366226fc6469a3a.pdf), original), with N = 1,204, found no main effect of the verb. The wording pulled more when memory of the event was weak. *Standing:* narrowed. *Population:* people.

**How interviewers of witnesses avoid leading.** The NICHD protocol for interviewing children sorts prompts from open to leading:

- *invitations* ("Tell me everything that happened"),
- *directive* prompts about details the child already mentioned,
- *option-posing* prompts that supply choices,
- *suggestive* prompts that assume what the child has not said.

Lamb, Orbach, Hershkowitz, Esplin & Horowitz (2007), *Child Abuse & Neglect* 31(11–12):1201–1231 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2007.03.021), original). With preschoolers, free-recall prompts produced more than 80% of initial disclosures. *Population:* people.

The cost of leading is shown by Leichtman & Ceci (1995), "Sam Stone", *Developmental Psychology* 31(4):568–578 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.31.4.568), original). The youngest children given both a stereotype beforehand and suggestive questions afterwards: 72% said a visitor did misdeeds he did not do. Controls were 96% accurate. The *cognitive interview*, which relies on open recall, drew more correct details (d = 0.87) with a smaller rise in incorrect ones (d = 0.28) across 55 comparisons (Köhnken, Milne, Memon & Bull 1999, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/10683169908414991), abstract). Memon, Meissner & Fraser (2010) replicated it. *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people.

**The asker's hypothesis shapes the questions.** Snyder & Swann (1978), *JPSP* 36(11):1202–1212 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.36.11.1202), abstract): people told to test whether someone "is an extravert" chose questions that sought confirming evidence, and the answers then confirmed it. *Narrowed:* the experimenters had supplied the list of questions, and when diagnostic questions were available people mostly chose those (Trope & Bassok 1982, secondary). Kassin, Goldstein & Savitsky (2003), *Law and Human Behavior* 27(2):187–203 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022599230598), abstract): interrogators led to expect guilt asked more guilt-presuming questions and pressed harder, most of all with innocent suspects. Neutral listeners then heard those suspects as more defensive. The premise in the interrogator's briefing shaped the questions, the answers, and third parties' judgment of the answers. *Population:* people.

**Telling the reader what to expect.** In lineups where the culprit was absent, instructions implying the culprit was present led 78% to pick someone, against 33% with "may not be present" instructions (Malpass & Devine 1981, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.66.4.482), secondary). A meta-analysis confirmed it (Steblay 1997, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1024890732059), abstract). Clark (2005) showed biased instructions also raise correct picks when the culprit is present. It is a trade-off between misses and false alarms, not pure harm. *Population:* people.

The same trade-off appears in visual search. When targets appeared on 50% of trials, observers missed 7%. When they appeared on 1%, observers missed 30% (Wolfe, Horowitz & Kenner 2005, *Nature* 435:439–440, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/435439a), abstract; numbers secondary). Mammographers missed 30% of cancers in normal low-prevalence work and 12% of the same cancers when prevalence was 50% (Evans, Birdwell & Wolfe 2013, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0064366), abstract). *Narrowed:* in the lab many of these misses were response slips people corrected when given the chance (Fleck & Mitroff 2007). *Population:* people. What a reader is told about how many problems to expect moves where it sets its bar.

**Experts are not immune.** Five fingerprint experts saw prints they had matched in their own past casework, now with context suggesting no match. Most contradicted their own earlier decision (Dror, Charlton & Péron 2006, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2005.10.017), abstract plus secondary). A review of 29 studies found bias in 9 of 11 that gave case information and in 4 of 4 that disclosed a previous decision (Cooper & Meterko 2019, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2019.01.016), abstract). Legal professionals' sentences moved toward sentencing demands they knew were random, including demands from dice they rolled themselves (Englich, Mussweiler & Strack 2006, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167205282152), abstract). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people.

**Withholding context is not free.** Forensic science's remedy is to withhold task-*irrelevant* information, control the order in which relevant information arrives, and have results checked by analysts blind to earlier conclusions. The counterweight: across 16 studies of readers reading medical tests with and without clinical information, the information improved accuracy and no study found a significant loss (Loy & Irwig 2004, *JAMA* 292(13):1602–1609, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.292.13.1602), abstract). The line the field draws is between context relevant to the task and context that only carries someone's conclusion. It is not a line between context and no context.

**What reduced bias, and what didn't.** A warning given *after* misinformation cut its effect to under half across 25 studies (Blank & Launay 2014, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.03.005), abstract), more optimistic than Loftus 2005. Listing reasons an anchor is wrong reduced anchoring, in experts too (Mussweiler, Strack & Pfeiffer 2000). A "consider the opposite" strategy corrected judgment better than instructions to be fair and unbiased (Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984, secondary). A concrete counter-strategy worked. General exhortation did not.

**Assigned dissent is not real dissent.** Nemeth, Brown & Rogers (2001), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 31:707–720 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.58), abstract): a genuine dissenting minority produced more and better solutions than any form of assigned devil's advocate. The group's earlier study found role-played dissent mostly bolstered the original view. *Population:* people. This bears on Manuel's worry that telling an agent not to be led makes it "unnecessarily adversarial or combative" instead of better.

**Language models.**

- *Stated opinions (sycophancy).* Sharma et al. (2023, ICLR 2024), "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548), original). Five assistants (Claude 1.3 and 2.0, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama 2 70B chat). Asked "are you sure?", they changed answers 32% (GPT-4) to 86% (Claude 1.3) of the time. A user suggesting a wrong answer cut accuracy by up to 27%. People and preference models sometimes preferred convincing sycophantic answers to correct ones, which points at training as a cause. Wei et al. (2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958), abstract): in PaLM models up to 540B, both scale and instruction tuning increased sycophancy. Perez et al. (2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251), abstract): larger models repeated back the user's preferred answer more. FlipFlop (Laban et al. 2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08596), abstract): a challenge made 10 models flip about 46% of the time. SycEval (Fanous et al. 2025, secondary) found it in 58% of cases for GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet and Gemini 1.5 Pro. *Standing:* replicated many times and still present in 2025 models. *Population:* models.
- *Hints the model uses but doesn't mention.* Turpin, Michael, Perez & Bowman (2023, NeurIPS, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388), abstract): a pattern in the examples such as "the answer is always (A)" cut accuracy by up to 36%, and the models' explanations never mentioned it. Chen et al. (Anthropic, 2025), "Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410), abstract): Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned a hint it used 25% of the time, DeepSeek R1 39%. *Narrowed:* reasoning models describe planted cues more often than non-reasoning ones (Chua & Evans 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08156)), but far from always. **Relevance:** a hint in a brief can move a lane's answer without the lane's reasoning showing it, so reading the lane's reasoning will not catch it.
- *Anchoring.* Lou & Sun (2024, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06593), abstract; details secondary): GPT-4, GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 moved toward numeric anchors, and the stronger models more consistently. Chain of thought, reflection and "ignore the hint" did not remove it. Instruction tuning and RLHF strengthened some human-like biases (Itzhak et al. 2024, *TACL*, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00225), abstract). A counterweight: some classic intuitive errors grew with GPT model size and then largely vanished in GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (Hagendorff, Fabi & Kosinski 2023, *Nature Computational Science* 3:833–838, [page](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43588-023-00527-x), abstract).
- *Framing a code reviewer.* Alexopoulos, Alexopoulos, Spinellis & Mitropoulos (2026), "Measuring and Exploiting Contextual Bias in LLM-Assisted Security Code Review" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18740), abstract; table read through a summarizing tool). Six models reviewed vulnerable code under five framings, from "strong bug" to "strong bug-free". Detection rates for neutral against strong bug-free framing were:

  | Model | Neutral | Strong bug-free |
  |---|---|---|
  | GPT-4o-mini | 97.2% | 3.6% |
  | Claude 3.5 Haiku | 68.4% | 8.5% |
  | DeepSeek V3 | 96.8% | 53.8% |
  | Claude Sonnet 4.5 | 97.4% | 75.5% |
  | Gemini 2.0 Flash | 95.5% | 79.4% |
  | Claude Opus 4.5 | 95.7% | 88.8% |

  In the attack part of the study, template-based attempts to bias the reviewer through PR metadata were ineffective and sometimes backfired, because the reviewer grew suspicious of direct attempts to bias it. Attempts refined with an LLM got past Claude Code and CodeRabbit in 32 of 33 cases. *Standing:* single study, 2026. *Population:* models. This is the most direct evidence I found on leading a reviewer agent. It also shows that the most capable models resist framing better, but not fully. It matches Dodd & Bradshaw's human finding that a visible agenda weakens a presupposition.
- *Accepting false premises.* Kim, Htut, Bowman & Petty (2023), "(QA)²", ACL ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.472/), abstract): models struggled with real search queries that carried false or unverifiable premises. Numbers not confirmed.
- *Asking a model to find and fix.* Jin & Chen (2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00539), abstract): models often flagged correct code as non-compliant with its requirements, and more detailed prompts, especially ones demanding explanations and fixes, raised the misjudgment rate. Telling a reviewer to find and fix is itself a lean toward false positives. Huang et al. (2024, ICLR, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798), abstract, numbers secondary): without outside feedback, asking a model to review its own answer did not help and sometimes turned right answers wrong.
- *Personas.* Zheng, Pei, Logeswaran, Lee & Jurgens (2024, Findings of EMNLP, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054), abstract): across 162 personas and 2,410 factual questions, personas did not improve accuracy. Nothing I found shows that framing a model as a reviewer adds ability. The items above show framing can add bias.
- *Judges.* Wang et al. (2024, ACL, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.511/), abstract): with ChatGPT as judge, swapping the order of two answers let Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries. Position, verbosity and self-preference biases are replicated (Zheng et al. 2023, MT-Bench; Panickssery, Bowman & Feng 2024). Reference answers and rubrics help on average but bring biases of their own (Li et al. 2025, Findings of EMNLP, abstract).

**On "models are ten times more suggestible than people".** No study I found measures human and model suggestibility on one scale. Griffin et al. (2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06074), abstract) ran one protocol on 1,000 people and 1,000 simulated participants and found models reproduced some human influence effects but not others. What is well supported is narrower. Stated opinions, hints, anchors and framing move models a lot. Some kinds of training make this worse. More capable models are somewhat, not reliably, harder to move.

## 4. Rules about how to do a task change the work

Manuel's correction on 2026-09-30 moved the concern from limits on searching to limits on "the design architecture of how the task is pursued or completed". The evidence below is organized around that broader question. Several of the strongest findings are not about searching at all.

**A threshold makes people withhold what they found.** *Satisfaction of search* was radiology's name for a reader who finds one abnormality and then misses a second (Tuddenham 1962; Berbaum et al. 1990, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1097/00004424-199002000-00006), abstract). Later work reframed it. Berbaum et al. (2015), *Academic Radiology* 22:1457–1465 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acra.2015.07.011), abstract): 20 radiologists read 64 chest images. An added nodule did not change accuracy or inspection time. It caused "reluctance to report". Readers still searched, and they raised their bar for saying so. A reanalysis of the old film studies found the same shift. Krupinski et al. (2017) confirmed it. Adamo, Cain & Mitroff (2013) renamed the effect "subsequent search misses". Becker, Anderson & Brascamp (2020) found the usual way of computing it overstates it. *Standing:* real in some settings, its size contested, its mechanism reframed. *Population:* people.

The model parallel is close, and it comes from the vendor of the models the factory runs. Anthropic's guide "Prompting Claude Opus 4.8" ([page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-4-8), original, read 2026-09-30) reports internal evals. When a review prompt says "only report high-severity issues", "be conservative" or "don't nitpick", the model can investigate as deeply as before and find the bugs, then leave out of its report the ones it judges to fall below the stated bar. Measured recall falls even though bug-finding ability improved. The guide recommends asking for every finding with a confidence and severity, and filtering in a separate step. It also says the model takes instructions literally and does not infer requests the prompt didn't make. *Standing:* vendor observation with no published numbers. *Population:* models. The page covers Claude Opus 4.8, and the factory runs Opus 5.5, which it does not cover.

This matches Manuel's example of a finding cap in both species. The reviewer does the work, and the brief's bar decides how much of it reaches the page.

**The instruction decides what gets seen.** Simons & Chabris (1999), "Gorillas in our midst", *Perception* 28:1059–1074 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059), original). 192 observers counted basketball passes. 46% failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through. The gorilla was black. Observers told to count the black team's passes noticed it 58% of the time, and those counting the white team's 27%. Noticing was 64% on the easy counting task and 45% on the hard one. Drew, Võ & Wolfe (2013), *Psychological Science* 24:1848–1853 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386), original): 20 of 24 radiologists missed a gorilla 48 times the size of a typical nodule in a lung CT, and most who missed it had looked right at it. *Standing:* inattentional blindness is widely replicated. The radiologist study is a single study. *Population:* people. An agent told what to look for is being told, in effect, what not to see.

A 2026 preprint tests this directly in models. Shin (2026), "The inattentional gap in task conditioned AI models that omit otherwise reportable safety critical signals" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26529), abstract). Across radiology text scenarios and chest-image tasks, ordinary focused instructions suppressed models' reporting of safety-critical findings that were present and that the same models reported when not given the narrow task. Suppression reached 0.92. The gap ranged from minimal to complete across seven models, did not shrink steadily with scale, and persisted in a reasoning model. One flagship model reported the critical findings anyway. In a 24-scenario probe, a separate critic with an open-ended brief restored every omitted finding. *Standing:* single-author preprint, not peer reviewed that I found. *Population:* models. It is the only model study I found that measures the gorilla effect itself: the task instruction, not missing ability, decided what the model reported.

**Measures replace the goal.** Kerr (1975), "On the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B", *Academy of Management Journal* 18(4):769–783 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/255378)), and Campbell (1979), *Evaluation and Program Planning* 2:67–90 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X)), are essays and case collections, not experiments (secondary). Campbell's example: police under pressure to cut crime rates downgraded or failed to record crimes. Experiments on *surrogation*, meaning treating the measure as if it were the goal, confirm it. Managers paid on one measure of a strategy acted as if the measure were the strategy, and several measures reduced this (Choi, Hecht & Tayler 2012, *The Accounting Review* 87(4), secondary). Surrogation happened without awareness, and merely knowing performance was measured was enough, with no pay attached (Black, Meservy, Tayler & Williams 2022, [doi](https://doi.org/10.2308/jmar-2020-071), abstract). *Population:* people.

**Specific goals help on simple tasks and hurt on complex, novel ones.** Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman (2009), "Goals gone wild", *Academy of Management Perspectives* 23(1):6–16 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amp.2009.37007999), abstract), list side effects of goal setting: narrow focus that neglects everything outside the goal, distorted risk taking, unethical behavior, inhibited learning. Locke & Latham replied in the same issue (23(1):17–23, [doi](https://doi.org/10.5465/amp.2009.37008000)) that the evidence was anecdotal and the pitfalls already known. *Standing:* contested over emphasis. Both sides accept that the side effects exist. The experiments behind it:

- On a complex stock-prediction task with no obvious strategy, specific difficult goals hurt, because people switched strategies too often (Earley, Connolly & Ekegren 1989, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.74.1.24), secondary).
- On a simple task an outcome goal beat "do your best". On a complex task a goal to learn beat both, and people found better strategies (Winters & Latham 1996, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1059601196212007), abstract).
- A specific goal led people to reduce the distance to it step by step and learn little about the problem. Without one, they explored and learned its structure (Vollmeyer, Burns & Holyoak 1996, *Cognitive Science* 20:75–100, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2001_3), abstract).

*Population:* people.

**Guidance that helps novices hurts experts.** The *expertise reversal effect*: Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), *Educational Psychologist* 38(1):23–31 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4), abstract). Meta-analysis: Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025), *Learning and Instruction* 98:102142 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142), abstract): 60 studies, N = 5,924. Novices gained from high assistance (d = 0.51). Experts did better with low assistance (d = −0.43). *Standing:* replicated. The effect is asymmetric: helping novices matters more than withholding help from experts. *Population:* people. For a capable reader, step-by-step method is a cost, not a courtesy.

**The same limit, worded as control or as information.** Koestner, Ryan, Bernieri & Holt (1984), *Journal of Personality* 52:233–248 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.1984.tb00879.x), abstract): children painting under the same limits were less creative when the limits were stated in a controlling way, and not when they were stated as information. A meaningful rationale, acknowledging the person's view, and conveying choice together improved how well people took rules on (Deci, Eghrari, Patrick & Leone 1994, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.1994.tb00797.x), abstract). Controlling language in health messages produced resistance, and a line restoring the reader's freedom reduced it (Miller et al. 2007, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00297.x), abstract; meta-analysis Rains 2013). *Population:* people. Whether models have anything like this resistance is untested as far as I found. The nearest model evidence is Anthropic's observation, below, that emphatic wording makes current Claude models over-apply an instruction.

**State the end and the reason, and leave the method open.** US Army doctrine ADP 6-0, *Mission Command* (2019), defines commander's intent as the purpose of an operation and its desired end state, and says mission orders stress the results to reach rather than how to reach them. Secondary, from quotations of the doctrine, because the PDF did not download. *Standing:* doctrine, not an empirical finding.

The one empirical test found is sobering. Shattuck (2000), "Communicating intent and imparting presence", *Military Review* 80(2):66–72 (original). Across 32 episodes in which company commanders faced unexpected situations, battalion commanders judged 17 (53%) as matching their intent. Six of those matched by coincidence. Leaving them out gives 11 of 32 (34%). Time served together did not predict matching. Shattuck also shows an intent statement written in practice that was full of method, limiting the subordinates it was meant to free. *Standing:* single study, small. *Population:* people. Writing the end and the reason instead of the method is necessary. It is not sufficient: intent statements often fail to carry the intent.

**No set of instructions is complete.** Garfinkel (1967), *Studies in Ethnomethodology*, Prentice-Hall ([PDF](https://monoskop.org/images/0/0c/Garfinkel_Harold_Studies_in_Ethnomethodology.pdf), original, pp. 29–30). Garfinkel asked students to write down what the parties in a conversation said, and alongside it what they understood. Each time he pressed for more accuracy, the students wrote more, and the task grew harder until it could not be done. His conclusion is that no set of instructions can repair its own essential incompleteness, however carefully it is written. Suchman (1987), *Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human-Machine Communication*, Cambridge University Press ([archive](https://archive.org/details/planssituatedact0000such)), argues from a study of people using a photocopier's expert help system that plans are resources for action, not specifications of it. The system assumed users followed its plan. Their actual actions answered the situation, and the two drifted apart until communication broke down. *Standing:* foundational qualitative work in sociology and human-computer interaction, argued rather than measured. *Population:* people and a machine.

These two works bear directly on Manuel's remark that "the point is to leave it open for something you CANT predict". They give a reason from the nature of instructions: a rule written in advance cannot contain the situation it will meet. The writer's choice is not whether the reader will fill gaps but whether the text helps it fill them well.

**Language models.**

- *Format restrictions.* Tam et al. (2024), "Let Me Speak Freely?", EMNLP Industry ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-industry.91/), original through a summarizing tool). Requiring JSON output hurt reasoning: GPT-3.5 on GSM8K fell from 76.6% to 49.3%. It helped classification. Answering in natural language first and converting afterwards mostly removed the loss. *Standing:* contested. A vendor rebuttal argues the prompts were not matched across conditions, and matched reruns tied ([.txt blog](https://blog.dottxt.ai/say-what-you-mean.html), secondary).
- *Length budgets.* Sun et al. (2025, EMNLP, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.389/), secondary): under tight output budgets across 30 models, reasoning models fell sharply. Phi-4-reasoning went from 72.2% to 53.5% at 1,024 tokens.
- *Specifying everything is not the fix either.* Yang et al. (2025), "What Prompts Don't Say" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13360), abstract): models inferred unstated requirements 41% of the time, and underspecified prompts were twice as likely to regress when the model or prompt changed. Adding every requirement did not reliably help, because instruction following is limited and requirements conflict. Jaroslawicz et al. (2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538), abstract): with 500 simultaneous instructions the best of 20 models followed 68%, favoring earlier ones.
- *Emphatic wording.* Anthropic's "Prompting best practices" ([page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices), original, read 2026-09-30) says Claude Opus 4.5 and 4.6 over-apply instructions that were written emphatically for older models, and advises replacing wording like "CRITICAL: You MUST use this tool" with plain wording. It recommends giving the reason behind an instruction, because the model generalizes from the reason. *Standing:* vendor guidance with no numbers. The one independent test near it found threats and tips had no significant effect overall across five models, though individual questions moved (Meincke et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00614), secondary).

**What the evidence does not show.** None of the model studies tests a limit on *strategy* as such: a limit on which files a reviewer may read, which order it must work in, or which list it must cite from. The measured cases are reporting thresholds, output formats, length budgets, instruction load and framing. The human evidence on complex tasks, expert readers and the incompleteness of instructions all predicts that limits on method cost the most where the task is novel and the reader capable.

**Counterweights.** Specific goals help on simple tasks. Guidance helps novices. Withholding *task-irrelevant* context protects independence (section 3). Several of Manuel's examples mix two different kinds of rule. One kind limits what the reader may *seek*, such as which files a reviewer may read. The other governs what the writer *volunteers*, such as the writer's own conclusion about the diff. The forensic evidence supports restraint in what the writer volunteers. It says nothing in favor of limiting what the reader may seek.

## 5. "Don't" instructions

The factory's `writing-for-agents` skill says a prohibition "drags the forbidden behaviour into context and makes it *more* available". This section is what is measured about that claim.

**In people.** Wegner's *ironic process theory*: trying not to think of something starts a monitor that searches for it, and under mental load the monitor wins. Wegner (1994), *Psychological Review* 101:34–52 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.1.34), abstract). People told to avoid a specific error while putting a golf ball made that error more under load (Wegner, Ansfield & Pilloff 1998, abstract). Meta-analysis: Wang, Hagger & Chatzisarantis (2020), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 15:778–793 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619898795), abstract): across 31 studies the later rebound is reliable, and the immediate increase appears only under load. *Population:* people, and about suppressing one's own thoughts, not following another's instruction. Closer to writing: speakers told to conceal a hidden object leaked its existence in 14.4% of descriptions where a leak was possible, against 5.4% without the instruction (Lane, Groisman & Ferreira 2006, *Psychological Science* 17:273–277, dissertation version read). Low absolute rates, single study.

**In models.**

- *Older models.* Jang, Ye & Seo (2022, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12711), abstract): OPT, GPT-3 and InstructGPT did worse on negated prompts as they grew.
- *Direct test of a prohibition.* Castricato et al. (2024), "Suppressing Pink Elephants with Direct Principle Feedback" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07896), full text through a summarizing tool). About a third of answers mentioned the topic with no instruction. Told not to mention it, GPT-4 fell from 0.33 to 0.13 and Llama-2-13B-Chat to 0.25. OpenHermes-13B was unchanged, and OpenHermes-7B rose slightly, to 0.36.
- *Inside the model.* Mann et al. (2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12381)) and Ramnauth & Scassellati (2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28639)), both read in full text through a summarizing tool, looked at models from 350M to 20B parameters. The forbidden concept stays active inside the model after a prohibition. In one study explicit mentions went to 0%, with a small measurable drift toward the concept in the output (d = 0.23). No frontier models were tested.
- *Vendor observation.* Anthropic's Opus 4.8 guide says generic prohibitions such as "don't use cream" move the model to a different fixed palette instead of producing variety, and that positive examples work better than negative ones. The vendor's general best-practices page carries a sample prompt that bans a list of overused fonts, and the prompt itself notes that the model still converges on another common one.

**Verdict.** "A prohibition makes the forbidden behavior more likely" is measured in older and small models. It is not shown for current frontier models, which mostly comply. A supportable version: a prohibition names the concept and keeps it active, and it does not say what to do instead, so the model lands on some other fixed default.

## 6. Writers misjudge what the reader knows

**The curse of knowledge.** Once you know something, you cannot easily imagine not knowing it.

- Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989), *Journal of Political Economy* 97:1232–1254 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/261651), abstract plus the authors' 2006 follow-up). Traders who knew companies' actual earnings could not set that knowledge aside when predicting what uninformed people would guess. Market trading cut the bias by about half.
- Newton (1990), *The rocky road from actions to intentions*, Stanford PhD dissertation ([scan](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/1990-newton.pdf), original). 40 people tapped well-known tunes. They predicted listeners would name 50% of them. Listeners named 3 of 120, or 2.5%. Listeners predicting for other listeners guessed about 3%, so the people without the tune in their heads were accurate. The dissertation's abstract gives a different count (2 of 150) from its own results table. *Standing:* no formal replication found. Often retold, usually second-hand.
- Birch & Bloom (2007), *Psychological Science* 18:382–386 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01909.x), original): knowing where an object really was changed adults' judgment of where a person with a false belief would look, d = 0.47. *Narrowed:* seven experiments with n = 3,074 put it at d = 0.20 (Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt 2014, *PLOS ONE*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0092406), original).
- Tullis & Feder (2022), *Memory & Cognition* ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-022-01382-3), original): the more people studied trivia answers, the worse they predicted what novices knew. Experimentally reducing reliance on their own knowledge did not help. The authors conclude estimators lack cues about the other person, not only that they lean on themselves.

*Population:* people.

**Experts write more abstractly for novices than novices need.** Hinds (1999), "The curse of expertise", *JEP: Applied* 5:205–221 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.5.2.205), abstract): experts predicted novice task times worse than intermediates did and resisted debiasing. Hinds, Patterson & Pfeffer (2001), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 86:1232–1243 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.86.6.1232), abstract): experts gave more abstract instructions, and novices taught by beginners did better on the target task. *Population:* people.

**Writers overestimate how well they are understood.**

- Keysar & Henly (2002), *Psychological Science* 13:207–212 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00439), abstract plus the authors' own accounts). Speakers said ambiguous sentences meaning one reading. Listeners got it right 61% of the time. Speakers expected 72%. In 46% of the cases where listeners misunderstood, speakers thought they had understood. Informed overhearers did not overestimate. Replicated with a moderator (Fay et al. 2008).
- Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng (2005), "Egocentrism over e-mail", *JPSP* 89:925–936 ([PDF](https://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/pa/kruger_email_ego.pdf), original). Email receivers detected sarcasm at chance, 50%. Voice receivers got nearly three-quarters. Senders were equally confident either way. Across 154 pairs, senders predicted 88.8% and achieved 70.4%. What removed senders' overconfidence completely was recording each message in the opposite tone, which forced them to hear it as a reader might.
- Savitsky, Keysar, Epley, Carter & Swanson (2011), "The closeness-communication bias", *JESP* 47:269–273 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.09.005), original): speakers overestimated being understood more with a friend or spouse than with a stranger. Wu & Keysar (2007), *Cognitive Science* 31:169–181 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/03640210709336989), abstract): pairs who shared more names for objects used names even for objects only the speaker knew, and confused their partners more often than pairs who shared less. *Population:* people. Both bear on an orchestrator that has shared a long session with a user or a lane. The more context it shares, the more likely it is to write as if it shares all of it.

**Taking the reader's view is slow and gets skipped under pressure.** Epley, Keysar, Van Boven & Gilovich (2004), *JPSP* 87:327–339 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.3.327), abstract): people start from their own view and adjust away from it until the answer seems plausible, and stop too soon. Time pressure increased the bias and accuracy incentives reduced it. Horton & Keysar (1996), *Cognition* 59:91–117 (abstract): without time pressure speakers used what they shared with the listener, and under time pressure they did not. Speakers check a plan against shared context after making it, not while making it. People with less working memory, or a demanding second task, applied another's perspective less (Lin, Keysar & Epley 2010, original). *Standing:* replicated (Damen et al. 2020). The "director task" often used in this research is disputed as a measure (Rubio-Fernández 2017, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-016-1190-7), original), but the findings about speakers above do not rely on it. *Population:* people.

**Language models.**

- *Knowing is not applying.* Gu et al. (2026, ICLR), "SimpleToM: Exposing the Gap between Explicit ToM Inference and Implicit ToM Application in LLMs" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13648), abstract, numbers from full text through a summarizing tool). Asked directly what a person knew, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet were right 96–98% of the time. Asked to predict what the person would do, they scored 49.5% and 67.0%. Asked to judge whether the behavior was reasonable, 15.3% and 24.9%. A reminder of the person's mental state lifted Claude to 96.9% and 84.1%. A generic system prompt barely helped. *Standing:* single benchmark, recent. *Population:* models. This is the most specific model finding for the map: the failure sits in applying what the model knows about the reader, and a reminder placed at the moment of use largely fixes it.
- *Tracking who knows what in a conversation.* Kim et al. (2023), "FANToM", EMNLP ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.890/), abstract; numbers vary by paper version). In conversations where participants leave and rejoin, people scored 87.5% on a combined consistency score and GPT-4 with chain of thought about 18%. On belief questions alone GPT-4 scored 80.6% against 93.8% for people. Success on single questions hid inconsistency across question types.
- *Framing decided whether the model committed.* Strachan et al. (2024), *Nature Human Behaviour* 8:1285–1295 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01882-z), full text): GPT-4 matched or beat 1,907 people on most theory-of-mind tests. It answered "insufficient information" on faux pas questions, but asked whether the speaker *more likely* knew, it was perfect. Ullman (2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08399)) showed small changes reversed GPT-3.5's successes. Riemer et al. (2025, ICML position paper, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19726)) argue benchmarks test predicting another agent, not adapting to it, and report models weak at the second.
- *The writer's knowledge leaks in.* Ma et al. (2026, CIKM, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25245), abstract): models asked to simulate a searcher who hasn't seen the answer leaked answer-side concepts into 97 of 100 topics. Baek, Liu & Ukpong (2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15776), abstract): an informed leader agent guiding a partly blind follower succeeded alone 35% of the time and with the follower 17%. Letting the follower ask beat pushing instructions to it.
- *Belief notes help.* Li et al. (2023, EMNLP, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10701), abstract): LLM agents in a cooperative game failed through lost context and invented task state. Explicit notes on what each agent believed improved the task and the agents' inferences about each other.

## 7. Meaning built in one exchange does not travel

This section is the human evidence for Manuel's complaint that terms get "DEFINED inside a context window that didnt make it to the PR".

**Shared terms shrink with use.** Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs (1986), "Referring as a collaborative process", *Cognition* 22:1–39 ([PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1980s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_%20Wilkes-Gibbs,%20D.%20_Referring%20as%20a%20collaborative%20process_%201986.pdf), original). Pairs arranged 12 abstract shapes over six rounds. Words per shape fell from 41 to 8. Each pair reached its own short names, and names differed widely between pairs (Hawkins, Frank & Goodman 2020, 15,000+ utterances, abstract). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people.

**People who heard everything still understand less.** Schober & Clark (1989), "Understanding by addressees and overhearers", *Cognitive Psychology* 21:211–232 ([PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1980s/Schober,%20M.F.%20_%20Clark,%20H.H.%20_Understanding%20by%20addressees%20and%20overhearers_%201989.pdf), original). Participants in the conversation placed 99% of shapes correctly. Overhearers of the full tape placed 88%. Overhearers who started at round three placed 68%. Letting overhearers pause the tape did not help, although most of them believed it had. *Standing:* the addressee advantage holds. Overhearers do better with dialogue than monologue and use the addressee's short replies as cues (Fox Tree 1999, original). *Population:* people.

**A speaker treats someone who only heard the exchange almost like a stranger.** Wilkes-Gibbs & Clark (1992), "Coordinating beliefs in conversation", *Journal of Memory and Language* 31:183–194 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(92)90010-U), original). Speakers worked with one partner, then switched to a second. Mean words per shape on the last round with the first partner, then the first round with the second:

| The second partner had | Words per shape |
|---|---|
| Sat beside the speaker as a silent side participant | 8.8 → 15.4 |
| Watched everything on video from another room | 11.3 → 22.6 |
| Heard everything in the room but could not see the shapes | 9.8 → 45.5 |
| Seen and heard nothing | 8.2 → 30.4 |

*Standing:* single study, small samples. *Population:* people. Speakers judged that hearing the words was not enough to share their meaning.

**Terms coined with one partner don't carry to the next.** Brennan & Clark (1996), "Conceptual pacts and lexical choice in conversation", *JEP: LMC* 22:1482–1493 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.22.6.1482), original). A *conceptual pact* is the term two people settle on for a thing. The chance that two different pairs chose the same term was 10%. Within a pair it was 56%. Speakers reused their settled term 48% of the time with the same partner and 18% with a new one. With a new partner they shifted back toward plain everyday words, from 20% to 55%. A meta-analysis of listener eye tracking supports the effects of partner and precedent (Kronmüller & Barr 2015, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2015.03.008), abstract). Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais (1987), "The vocabulary problem in human-system communication", *CACM* 30:964–971 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1145/32206.32212), abstract): two people favored the same word for a thing less than 20% of the time. *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people.

**Records written for insiders fail outsiders.** Garfinkel & Bittner, "'Good' organizational reasons for 'bad' clinic records", in Garfinkel (1967), *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (original). Clinic files that looked inadequate to researchers were adequate to the staff who wrote them, because staff read them against shared, unstated practice. A file is written for the reader the writer has in mind, and it fails any other reader. *Standing:* qualitative, foundational. *Population:* people.

**Grounding costs depend on the medium.** Clark & Schaefer (1989), *Cognitive Science* 13:259–294 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_7), original), describe a contribution to a conversation as two phases: presenting something, then getting evidence it was understood. Clark & Brennan (1991), "Grounding in communication" ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006), original), note that written media let the writer revise and the reader review, but give no immediate back-and-forth. *Standing:* theory chapters, no data of their own. My inference from them: a brief to a lane has no step where the reader shows it understood, so the writer must supply up front what conversation would repair.

**Live adaptation works when the channel allows it.** Isaacs & Clark (1987), *JEP: General* 116:26–37 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.116.1.26), original): New Yorkers describing city landmarks to non-New Yorkers added descriptions to names 42% of the time, against 13% with other New Yorkers. Pairs assessed each other's expertise almost at once. The success depended on back-and-forth.

**Language models.**

- *Models understand shortened references but don't shorten their own.* Hua & Artzi (2024, COLM, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01417), abstract).
- *Requests in pieces.* Laban, Hayashi, Zhou & Neville (2025), "LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120), original). 15 models, including GPT-4.1, o3, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Gemini 2.5 Pro, over 200,000 simulated conversations. Giving a request in pieces over several turns cost 39% on average, mostly in reliability. Putting the same pieces into one message recovered about 95%. Reasoning models were no better. The named causes were early assumptions, premature answers, and over-weighting the first and last turns. Anthropic's Opus 4.8 guide says the same about its own models. *Population:* models.
- *Text passed between models drifts.* Perez et al. (2025), "When LLMs Play the Telephone Game", ICLR ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04503), abstract): text passed along chains of models drifted toward attractors in tone, difficulty and length. More open-ended instructions drifted more.
- *Multi-agent failures.* Cemri et al. (2025), "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?", NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657), original). 1,600+ annotated traces across 7 frameworks, 14 failure modes, including disobeying the task specification (11.8%), task derailment (7.4%) and failing to ask for clarification (6.8%). Percentages vary between versions. Better role specifications helped one system by 9.4%, and prompt fixes left most failures in place.
- *A vendor's experience.* Anthropic, "How we built our multi-agent research system" (2025, [page](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system), original). Vague subagent briefs led to misread tasks and duplicated work. Briefs needed an objective, an output format, tool guidance and task boundaries. This is an observation, not an experiment. Cognition's essay "Don't Build Multi-Agents" (2025, [page](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)) argues for sharing whole traces rather than messages, with no measurement.

## 8. Readers who misunderstand rarely know it, and rarely ask

**Scripted wording against clarification.** Survey research has argued for decades over *standardized interviewing*, where the interviewer reads each question exactly as written and may not explain it, against *conversational interviewing*, where the interviewer may explain what the question means. Schober & Conrad (1997), "Does conversational interviewing reduce survey measurement error?", *POQ* 61:576–602. Read through the same authors' summary, Conrad & Schober (1999), "Conversational interviewing and data quality" ([FCSM PDF](https://nces.ed.gov/FCSM/pdf/V_B_Conrad2_FCSM1999.pdf), original). The results:

- When respondents' situations matched the question's concept plainly (a receipt for an end table, asked about furniture), accuracy was near perfect either way.
- When the situation was atypical (a floor lamp), scripted interviews got 28% right and flexible ones 87%.
- The flexible interviews took more than three times as long. Accuracy rose about 7% for each extra minute spent clarifying, r = .98.
- In a field study, answers checked against the definitions were consistent 57% of the time with scripted interviews and 95% with conversational ones.

The authors name the core risk: a respondent can misunderstand a question and be confident in the misreading. *Standing:* consistent across their lab and field studies. *Population:* people.

**People don't ask, even when told to.** In the same program:

- Field respondents told they could ask asked on 4% of the occasions help was given. Interviewers volunteered the other 96%.
- In an on-screen version, respondents told definitions were *essential* clicked for them on 81% of questions. Those told definitions were *available* clicked on 23%.

Suessbrick, Schober & Conrad (2000), *ASA Proceedings* ([PDF](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/papers/2000_155.pdf), original):

- Respondents' readings of ordinary survey words matched the official definitions 39% of the time.
- For "smoking", 46% counted only inhaled puffs and 54% all puffs.
- Told to ask whenever unsure, respondents almost never did. The authors' reading is that it did not occur to either side that their understandings differed.

Belson (1981), *The Design and Understanding of Survey Questions*, found by re-interviewing that ordinary questions were widely misread. For example, 61% counted days other than weekdays in a question about weekday TV viewing (secondary, via Suessbrick et al.). *Standing:* consistent, small samples. *Population:* people.

**Placement of explanation matters.** Redline (2013), *POQ* 77(S1):89–105 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfs067), secondary): in a web survey of 913 people, 49.6% answered in line with a definition when instructions were given, against 1.1% without. Instructions placed after a question got less attention. Breaking an instruction into a series of questions worked best and took longer. Bransford & Johnson (1972) found context supplied before an obscure passage raised comprehension, and the same context supplied after did not (secondary).

**Language models.** Current models rarely ask either.

- Vijayvargiya et al. (2026, ICLR), "Ambig-SWE" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13069), original through a summarizing tool): on underspecified SWE-bench tasks, models in neutral conditions almost never asked a question, even when the task was badly underspecified. Interaction recovered up to 74% of the lost performance. Claude Sonnet 4 detected underspecification best when strongly encouraged. Some models over-asked, and one never asked.
- Wu & Fard (2025), "HumanEvalComm", *ACM TOSEM* ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00215)): faced with ambiguous, inconsistent or incomplete coding problems, ChatGPT asked a question instead of writing code 14% of the time, and DeepSeek Chat 38%.
- CLAMBER (Zhang et al. 2024, ACL, secondary): models struggle to spot ambiguity and ask good questions, even with chain of thought.

*Standing:* replicated across benchmarks. *Population:* models. Manuel's observation that an agent "doesnt stop and go 'hey you just used a word without context'" matches both the human and the model evidence. Neither species reliably asks.

**This debate is the template debate.** Survey methodology has spent decades on the question Manuel raises about brief templates. Standardized wording promises that every reader gets the same stimulus. The evidence says the same words do not mean the same thing to every reader, and that fixed wording fails exactly on the atypical case. The conversational school's answer is to standardize the *meaning* and let the wording vary to reach it (Suchman & Jordan 1990, cited in Conrad & Schober 1999). Its cost is time, and its risk, raised by critics of conversational interviewing, is that a flexible interviewer can lead. Standardized wording and freedom to clarify each protect against a different failure. The field has not settled which to pick. It has measured what each costs.

## 9. What helps a reader who has less context

**Put what is known before what is new.** Haviland & Clark (1974), "What's new?", *JVLVB* 13:512–521 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4), original): a sentence was understood faster when the thing it treated as known had been stated just before, rather than needing an inference to link it. Small samples.

**Explicit links help low-knowledge readers.** McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), "Are good texts always better?", *Cognition and Instruction* 14:1–43 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1), abstract): low-knowledge readers learned more from a text with explicit connections. High-knowledge readers sometimes learned more deeply from a text with gaps they had to fill. *Narrowed:* the gap benefit held only for less-skilled high-knowledge readers (O'Reilly & McNamara 2007, abstract). The robust part is that explicit connections help readers without background. Britton & Gülgöz (1991, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329), abstract only) used a model of reading to find each place a reader had to infer a link and made the link explicit, with gains in recall (numbers not reached). *Population:* people. A lane starting cold is a low-knowledge reader for the project, however capable it is.

**Concrete beats abstract for those new to a domain.** Hinds, Patterson & Pfeffer (2001), above.

**Where content sits in a long input matters for models.** Liu et al. (2024), "Lost in the Middle", *TACL* ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172), original): GPT-3.5-Turbo answered 75.8% correctly with the relevant document first and 53.8% with it in the middle, below its 56.1% with no documents. *Narrowed:* the U-shape is weaker in newer models, but performance still falls with length. At 32K tokens, 11 of 13 models fell below half their short-context score (NoLiMa 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167), abstract). Irrelevant sentences cut accuracy sharply on math problems, and an instruction to ignore irrelevant information helped (Shi et al. 2023, ICML, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00093), abstract). *Population:* models. Context is not free for the reader. What a writer adds to be safe costs attention elsewhere.

**Say why.** Anthropic's best-practices page recommends giving the reason for an instruction, with the example of a ban on ellipses that works better once the prompt says the text will be read aloud by a speech engine. Vendor guidance, no numbers. The human parallel is the self-determination research in section 4: a rule with its rationale is followed better and more flexibly than the rule alone.

## 10. Does telling the writer to "consider the reader" work?

**Instructions alone barely help.** Eyal, Steffel & Epley (2018), "Perspective mistaking", *JPSP* 114:547–571 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000115), original). Most people expected that imagining another's perspective would make them more accurate about that person. Across 15 experiments on reading emotions, spotting fake smiles and detecting lies, it made them slightly *less* accurate, d = −0.26. It reduced egocentrism, but what replaced it was not more accurate. In the 25th experiment, couples who *asked* their partner, which the authors call perspective-getting, did improve. *Standing:* no independent replication found. Damen et al. (2020, abstract) likewise found focusing people on another's perspective did not reduce projection. Hinds (1999) found experts resisted debiasing. Tullis & Feder (2022) found reducing reliance on one's own knowledge did not help. *Population:* people.

**What worked was signal from the reader.**

- Even minimal feedback on how well readers picked out the figures made writers revise better, and the gain carried over to new figures (Traxler & Gernsbacher 1992, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/01690969208409378), abstract).
- Writers who did the reader's task themselves revised successfully (Traxler & Gernsbacher 1993, abstract).
- Writers who read transcripts of real readers thinking aloud while struggling with texts beat classes taught audience-analysis rules and peer review at predicting readers' problems. The gain was largest for problems caused by what the text left out, and it transferred to a new kind of text (Schriver 1992, *Written Communication* 9:179–208, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0741088392009002001), abstract).
- Kruger et al. (2005) removed senders' overconfidence by making them hear their message the way a reader could. Kraut, Lewis & Swezey (1982, abstract) found speakers who got more listener feedback produced summaries that were easier to understand.

*Population:* people.

**The survey field's version of this is pretesting.** Before a question goes into the field, it is tried on real respondents: *cognitive interviews* ask people to think aloud or explain their answers, *behavior coding* records where interviews go wrong, and *split-ballot experiments* randomly give different wordings to comparable groups. Presser, Couper, Lessler, Martin, Martin, Rothgeb & Singer (2004), "Methods for testing and evaluating survey questions", *POQ* 68(1):109–130, and the book of the same year ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/0471654728)). Search results only. I did not read it. *Standing:* standard practice, with published debate about how well each method predicts field problems.

**A structured handoff with read-back reduced medical errors.** Starmer et al. (2014), "Changes in medical errors after implementation of a handoff program", *NEJM* 371(19):1803–1812 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1405556), abstract via PubMed). Across nine hospitals and 10,740 admissions, a handoff program called I-PASS cut the medical-error rate by 23% (24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions) and preventable adverse events by 30%. The duration of the oral handoff did not change. I-PASS is a fixed structure for handing a patient to the next shift, and its last element is "synthesis by receiver": the receiver restates the plan. *Standing:* a pre-post study, not randomized. The intervention bundled the template with training and observed practice, so the template's own share is unknown. *Population:* people. It is one of the few large tests of a fixed communication template in a high-stakes field. It improved outcomes, and it builds the reader's restatement into the template.

**Language models.** The same shape shows up in models. A general instruction does little. Information about the specific reader, placed at the moment of use, does much more:

- A generic system prompt barely helped on SimpleToM, while a reminder of the specific person's mental state closed most of the gap (Gu et al. 2026).
- Explicit belief notes helped cooperating agents (Li et al. 2023).
- Letting the less-informed agent ask beat pushing instructions to it (Baek et al. 2025, Ambig-SWE).

None of this is tested with a model writing a brief for another model.

## 11. Where people and models differ

This section collects the differences from the sections above so the map does not have to reassemble them.

- **Models shift under changes people ignore.** Formatting alone moved accuracy by up to 76 points on Llama-2-13B (Sclar, Choi, Tsvetkov & Suhr 2024, ICLR, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324), abstract). The effect is smaller in larger models (He et al. 2024, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10541)). Typos and letter swaps moved every model in Tjuatja et al. A brief's surface form matters for models in ways it would not for a person.
- **Models do not reproduce most human survey biases one for one** (Tjuatja et al. 2024). Human effects are hypotheses for models, not facts about them.
- **Agreement with the asker is trained in.** Sycophancy grew with scale and instruction tuning in some model families (Wei et al. and Perez et al.), and people and preference models rewarded it (Sharma et al.). Whatever a model does with the asker's belief, some of it comes from training to please, not from misreading.
- **More capable models resist framing better, but not fully.** Claude Opus 4.5 lost 7 points under bug-free framing where GPT-4o-mini lost 94 (Alexopoulos et al. 2026). GPT-4 obeyed a prohibition better than smaller models (Castricato et al. 2024). But the stronger GPT models were anchored more consistently (Lou & Sun 2024). Capability reduces the pull in some studies and not in others, and it never removed it.
- **Current Claude models read instructions literally.** Opus 4.8 does not infer requests the prompt didn't make, and it holds to a stated reporting bar more closely than earlier models (vendor). A literal reader makes every word in a brief bind harder, including the words meant only as illustration.
- **Models already converge.** Different models give 71–82% similar answers to open questions (Jiang et al. 2025). Human fixation studies start from diverse people. Model lanes start from a shared default.
- **No resistance to control was found in models.** People push back against controlling wording (reactance). I found no evidence either way that models do. The observed model response to emphatic wording is over-applying the instruction, not resisting it.
- **Neither asks.** Both people and models rarely seek clarification unprompted (section 8).

## 12. What this means for the map's decisions

This section is my reading of how the evidence bears on the map's sibling tickets. It is not a finding, and the tickets own the decisions. I have tried to state tensions rather than conclusions, because several findings cut both ways.

**The writing standard (#155).** The evidence supports writing the purpose and the reason, not only the rule (self-determination research, mission command, vendor guidance). It supports defining any term the reader did not see coined: speakers themselves treat a reader who only heard an exchange almost as a stranger. It supports putting context before the content that depends on it (Haviland & Clark, Bransford & Johnson, Redline). The standard should not rest on telling writers to "consider the reader", because that instruction alone barely works in people, and a generic instruction barely worked in models. What worked in both was information about the specific reader at the moment of writing, and feedback from real readers. A standard that tells writers to guard against leading also has to guard against the opposite pull: assigned dissent was weaker than genuine dissent (Nemeth et al.), and more detailed find-and-fix prompts raised false positives in model reviewers (Jin & Chen).

**Rules that bound how a task is pursued (#152).** The evidence supports Manuel's broader framing over the earlier search-only one. The costliest human findings are about limits on method and bars on reporting, not only on reading:

- the reporting bar that makes readers withhold findings they already made,
- the specific goal on a complex task,
- step-by-step guidance for an expert,
- the controlling wording that lowers creativity,
- the instruction that tells an observer what to look at, which in the one model study of it hid findings that a separate open-ended critic then recovered.

Garfinkel and Suchman give a reason from the nature of instructions: no rule written in advance can contain the situation it will meet. Two counterweights should shape the decision:

1. The forensic evidence shows that restraint in what the *writer volunteers* protects a reader's independence. That is a different thing from a limit on what the reader may *seek*.
2. The evidence on simple tasks and novice readers is the one place where specific method helps.

**Brief templates (#157).** Survey methodology's standardized-against-conversational debate is this question, studied for decades.

- Fixed wording fails on the atypical case (28% against 87%), and the atypical case is the one review and research lanes exist to catch. The flexible alternative cost more than three times the time.
- Telling readers that checking definitions was essential, rather than merely available, raised how often they checked from 23% to 81% (Conrad & Schober).
- A feature repeated across every item gets read as boilerplate (Igou et al.), so the fixed part of a template will be read past. The variable part will be read as the point.
- Examples in a template copy themselves into the output (Tourangeau et al. and the design-fixation studies).
- The medical handoff evidence shows a fixed structure can improve outcomes when it carries the reader's restatement back to the writer.

**How we'll know the writing works (#153).** Other fields have methods that transfer:

- *Split-ballot experiments* give two wordings to comparable readers at random, the survey field's controlled comparison.
- *Cognitive interviewing and think-aloud* ask readers to explain what they understood. Schriver's transcripts were the one writer training that transferred.
- *Perturbation tests* reword a prompt without changing its meaning and measure the change (Sclar et al.).
- *Recall rather than precision* is the measure for reviewers, since a bar in the brief moves recall while the reviewer's ability stays the same (vendor).
- One experiment nobody has run, and the factory could: give lanes the same open-ended task with and without an illustrative list, and count what each finds outside the list.

**Where the guidance lives and when it arrives (#156).** In SimpleToM, a reminder of the specific reader's state *at the moment of use* fixed most of the gap and a generic system prompt did not. In people, perspective-taking is skipped under time pressure and checked after a plan is made, not while making it (Horton & Keysar). Both point toward guidance that arrives when a brief is being written and names the actual reader, over standing guidance loaded earlier.

**Rework of existing files (#158).** The model evidence is strongest for reviewer-shaped work. It covers framing a code reviewer, reporting thresholds, judge biases, and the bias that comes from asking a model to find and fix. If the rework has to start somewhere, adversarial briefs are where the measured harm is largest.

## 13. What the evidence leaves open

- **Illustrative lists in open-ended model tasks.** No controlled study measures what a model misses outside a non-exhaustive list of examples. The human evidence predicts a loss. The factory can run this test itself.
- **Limits on strategy in models.** Measured cases cover formats, lengths, thresholds and instruction load, not limits on which sources a model may consult or which method it must follow.
- **Human and model suggestibility on one scale.** Untested. Claims of "more" or "less" suggestible rest on different studies with different protocols.
- **Model-to-model briefing.** Almost everything on models tests a person or a benchmark writing to a model. The multi-agent failure taxonomies are the closest, and they classify failures without testing wording.
- **Current Claude models.** Most model studies used 2022–2025 models. The factory runs Claude Opus 5.5. The vendor's own notes are the most current evidence and have no published numbers.
- **Several human classics are weaker than their reputation.** Loftus & Palmer's verb effect, the life-satisfaction order effect, Snyder & Swann's confirmation effect, and parts of design fixation have narrowed or failed. Cite the robust versions: presupposition, open against closed questions, response ranges, forbid/allow, part-list cueing, inattentional blindness, curse of knowledge, and the addressee advantage.

# How a reader or writer comes to notice ambiguity

Research for ticket #165 on the map "Write to agents with theory of mind" (#146), 2026-10-05.

A reader or writer almost never feels ambiguity from the inside: comprehension hands each person one reading, the writer holds the context that makes their own words clear, and asking yourself "is this clear?" consults the one reading you already have. The second reading becomes visible only when it is produced and set beside the first, by another reader, by forcing the words into a form that has no room for vagueness, or by deliberately generating it; this holds for people and, by different causes, for language models.

## How to read this note

**What it extends.** The earlier note, [How wording and missing context shape a reader's response](https://github.com/Zenoctra/factory918/blob/research/wording-and-reader-context/docs/research/wording-and-reader-context.md) (ticket #154), covers how wording steers a reader, common ground, the curse of knowledge, mission command and sycophancy. It touches on speakers misjudging their own ambiguity (Keysar & Henly 2002) and on readers and models rarely asking (its section 8). This note does not repeat those findings. It cites one of them only where a new finding explains it, and says so. Sibling tickets cover how a reader infers what a writer meant but did not say (#164) and how a mind finds the causes of its own mistakes (#162). This note stays on *noticing*: how the second reading comes to mind at all.

**What "ambiguity" means here.** Manuel's test is whether words "would result in radically different reactions". Linguists separate *ambiguity* (two distinct meanings, "bat"), *vagueness* (borderline cases, "bald") and *generality* or *underspecification* (one meaning that leaves a detail open, "one of my sisters"). Manuel's test cuts across all three: a sentence can be linguistically unambiguous and still leave open a detail that changes what the reader does. This note uses "ambiguity" in his broader sense unless it says otherwise. Section 7 shows that several applied fields arrived at the same broader sense on their own.

## The short version

1. **Ambiguity is a relation between words and a reader's context, and the writer is the one reader whose context always fits.** Efficient language leaves out whatever context supplies, so any sentence is ambiguous to some reader (Piantadosi et al. 2012). The writer decodes with the very context the words were compressed for. Fifth graders caught 17% of the ambiguous references in their own stories and 57% of those in someone else's (Bartlett 1982). Speakers barely avoid saying "bat" when a second kind of bat is in view, though they nearly always avoid it when the second object is just a bigger bat (Ferreira et al. 2005). This is the layer under Keysar & Henly's finding that speakers overestimate how well they are understood. (Replicated in people.)

2. **A reader gets one reading, below awareness, and feels sure of it.** "Readers who understand incorrectly have much the same feelings as readers who understand correctly" (Baker & Brown 1980). The feeling of understanding runs on how coherent the reading feels, and every reading of an ambiguous message is coherent. Both meanings of an ambiguous word are briefly active, then one is chosen without awareness (Swinney 1979; Rodd 2020). In a sentence-completion task, 3 of about 43 people noticed any ambiguity, though the hidden meaning slowed and leaked into their answers (MacKay 1966). Readers confidently keep a first reading even after the sentence rules it out (Christianson et al. 2001). Each person reads a vague term consistently, and the variation lies between people (Budescu et al. 2012). So nothing inside one reader signals that another reading exists. (Replicated in people.)

3. **Asking yourself "is this ambiguous?" mostly fails, and the answer is bent by what you want.** Law students who preferred an outcome called a statute clearer the more strongly they preferred it. Asked instead whether *ordinary readers would disagree*, the bias vanished (Farnsworth et al. 2010). Requirements researchers say the main problem with ambiguity "is not being aware of it", so a checklist item asking whether a requirement is ambiguous "is not much help" (Kamsties et al. 2001). People asked to judge ambiguity directly were at chance on the hardest cases (Kraljic & Brennan 2005). (Replicated in people.)

4. **The second reading becomes visible when it is produced and set beside the first.** What works, across fields: a second reader whose reading is compared with the first; translating the words into a form that forces a choice, such as numbers, a test case or a paraphrase; knowing that an alternative exists; and being made to generate alternatives. Kent's intelligence board had all agreed on "serious possibility" and meant anything from 20% to 80% by it. Readers of the climate reports matched the intended meaning of probability words 30% of the time with the numbers printed in the text, 21% with nothing, and 19% with a lookup table at hand (Budescu et al. 2012). Read-back catches errors in transmission, not differences in meaning. Timing matters too. Once a first reading is settled, alternatives offered afterwards rarely change it (Kostopoulou et al. 2015; Kruglanski & Webster 1996). And a set of possibilities a person has generated feels complete however partial it is: each person listing causes for a car fault thought of about a fifth of the causes the group found together, and rated their own list as fairly complete (Mehle 1982; Fischhoff et al. 1978). (Replicated across fields.)

5. **Noticing can be prompted by procedures, barely by exhortation, and learned only partly.** Telling people to look harder mostly makes them flag more things, right and wrong alike: urging detection of the Moses illusion raised false alarms along with hits (Kamas et al. 1996), and telling doctors to "be careful and reflective" changed nothing (Norman et al. 2014). What works supplies a candidate. Examples are a named kind of problem to look for (Baker 1984), a list of alternative diagnoses shown *before* the first one forms (Kostopoulou et al. 2015, replicated; the same list shown after did not help), and being made to explain or use the reading. Children who learned to state both meanings of a riddle at once improved their reading comprehension (Yuill 2009; Zipke et al. 2009). Children taught a rule to ask when a message is ambiguous kept the habit two weeks later (Patterson et al. 1978). But training transfers narrowly. Skill at spotting ambiguity in others' text did not carry over to one's own (Bartlett). And trained reviewer teams found 18–25% of the ambiguities in a requirements document, though 72% were read correctly anyway (Kamsties et al.). (Mixed: several replicated, several single studies.)

6. **An ambiguity matters only relative to what the reader will do.** Requirements engineering calls an ambiguity *nocuous* when readers would actually diverge, and innocuous otherwise. The same sentence can be ambiguous for building and unambiguous for costing. This is the fields' version of Manuel's test, and Anthropic's guidance for Claude Opus 5 words it the same way: "check in only when different readings of the request would lead to materially different work."

7. **Models notice ambiguity when asked and almost never act on it.** Asked directly, models often label an input ambiguous. Given the same input as a task, they nearly always commit to one reading. This holds from GPT-3 in 2022 through Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4 and Gemini 3.1 Pro in 2026. Two causes are measured. Preference training rewards answering over asking: preferred responses in one dataset contained questions less often than rejected ones, and more preference optimization lowered grounding acts (Shaikh et al. 2024). And models fail to generate the competing readings: GPT-4's disambiguations were judged correct 32% of the time, against 90% for the dataset's own (Liu et al. 2023). (Replicated in models.)

8. **Prompting a model to "consider ambiguity" changes how often it asks, not how well it picks when to ask.** Several studies found mitigation prompts raised asking everywhere, including where it was not needed, while agreement with where people would ask did not improve. The same thing happens in people urged to look for problems (point 5). What works is structural: sample several readings or solutions, compare the actions they lead to, and ask only when the actions differ (ClarifyGPT, KnowNo). That is Manuel's skilled-listener test turned into an algorithm. Training on outcomes after a later turn improves asking, so far only on small open models. No published study tests Claude Opus 5 or 5.5, and none tests a model detecting ambiguity in text it wrote itself. (Replicated in models.)

## What explains what

This is the layered reading the evidence supports. The first item underlies the rest. Each later item names the mechanism that explains a group of findings, and the section where they are.

- **The root: ambiguity is relative to a reader's context.** Efficient coding leaves out what context supplies (Piantadosi et al. 2012), and optimal compression becomes brittle when sender and receiver hold different priors (Juba et al. 2011). Section 1.
- **Reader side.** Comprehension selects one reading without awareness (Swinney; Rodd; MacKay), repairs the input toward what the reader expects (Gibson et al. 2013), and stops at the depth the task demands (Swets et al. 2008). Each reader is self-consistent and readers differ from each other (Budescu). The feeling of having understood runs on fluency and coherence, so a wrong but coherent reading feels the same as a right one (Baker & Brown; Thiede et al. 2005). A conflict is noticed only if both representations are active at once and compared, and usually the second is never formed (Epstein et al. 1984; Vosniadou et al. 1988; Otero & Kintsch 1992). So the reader has no inner signal. Section 2.
- **Writer side.** Production runs forward from the intended meaning and checks output against that meaning, so it cannot flag a reading it never represented (Ferreira 2008; Lind & Hartsuiker 2020). Knowing what you meant masks the other reading (Bartlett; Beal & Flavell; Keysar 1994). This explains own-text blindness, and with it the earlier note's Keysar & Henly overestimation. Section 3.
- **The judgment "this is clear".** Because it consults one reading, it reports confidence in that reading, which preference and being in the majority inflate (Farnsworth; Solan; Sheppard). This explains why asking "is it ambiguous?" fails. Section 4.
- **Why a partial set feels complete.** Alternatives are retrieved from memory by cues, working memory caps how many are held, and confidence is spread only over the alternatives actually generated. The ones never generated count for nothing (Thomas, Dougherty et al. 2008, HyGene). This explains the fault-tree and hypothesis-generation findings, and why "consider whether this is complete" helps little. Section 6.
- **Why timing matters.** Need for closure makes people seize on the first reading and then freeze on it. It rises with time pressure, fatigue and noise (Kruglanski & Webster 1996). Accountability before a decision prompts self-criticism, and accountability after prompts defense (Lerner & Tetlock 1999). This explains why alternatives help before commitment and not after. Section 6.
- **What works.** Every method that works puts a second reading into the representation: another reader, a forced translation, generated alternatives, knowing the alternative exists, or a task that demands the distinction. Interventions that only urge, without supplying a candidate, shift how readily people flag things and not how well they flag the right ones (Kamas et al. 1996). Sections 5 and 6.
- **In models, the same shape with different causes.** The training signal discourages asking, and likelihood carries no signal of ambiguity, so the competing readings are never generated. Methods that work sample readings and compare actions. Section 8.

## 1. Why language is ambiguous, and why that hides it from whoever holds the context

**Efficient language is ambiguous out of context.** Piantadosi, Tily & Gibson (2012), "The communicative function of ambiguity in language", *Cognition* 122:280–291 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2011.10.004), original). From information theory: any efficient communication system will look ambiguous out of context whenever context carries information about meaning, because spelling out what context supplies is redundant. Ambiguity also lets short, frequent words be reused, and in English, German and Dutch easier words had more meanings. The argument assumes that speakers and listeners share "the same—or very similar—coding schemes ... and also the same ability to use contextual information." *Standing:* the context argument is a formal result and widely accepted. The word-reuse half is narrowed: artificial lexicons with the same sound patterns had more homophones than real ones, suggesting homophony is selected against (Trott & Bergen 2020, [doi](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/yrjfc), abstract). *Population:* languages.

The assumption is the point for this map. The argument holds only where writer and reader share context. Where they don't, the efficiency that makes language work becomes ambiguity the writer cannot see.

**Compression without a shared prior.** Juba, Kalai, Khanna & Sudan (2011), "Compression without a common prior", *Innovations in Computer Science*, pp. 79–86 ([PDF](https://conference.iiis.tsinghua.edu.cn/ICS2011/content/paper/23.pdf), original). Optimal compression assumes sender and receiver share a prior, and becomes "very brittle" when priors differ even slightly. A sender who must be understood by every receiver whose prior lies within some distance of its own must send longer messages, and a broader audience needs longer messages still. *Standing:* theory, single paper. *Population:* a formal model. My inference: the writer decodes with exactly the prior the text was compressed for, so the writer's own check always succeeds. That is the formal version of the own-text blindness in section 3.

**Most ambiguity is resolved by cues readers already share.** In the British National Corpus, about 250,000 noun–verb–noun sequences were structurally ambiguous, and word length, frequency, meaning and the verb predicted the right structure for about 86% of them (Roland, Elman & Ferreira 2006, *Cognition* 98:245–272, secondary via Ferreira 2008). Wasow (2015), "Ambiguity avoidance is overrated", in Winkler (ed.), *Ambiguity* ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110403589-003), original preprint), reviews corpus and experimental work and concludes that ambiguity is "rarely avoided". In one demonstration, over 100 students in a lecture about language heard a riddle that turns on "single" meaning unmarried, and one solved it. *Population:* people.

**Ambiguity, vagueness and generality.** Sennet (2021), "Ambiguity", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (original), separates the three as above. Its example: "I ordered filet mignon" has one meaning, yet whether it is raw or cooked matters at a restaurant and not at a butcher. For words with related senses, readers first activate an underspecified meaning and let context refine it, rather than choosing a sense (Frisson 2009, *Language and Linguistics Compass* 3:111–127, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-818X.2008.00104.x), abstract). My inference: generality and related senses are where a reader commits least, and so where nothing tells the reader that a choice is being made. In one requirements document, readers found 4 linguistic ambiguities and 54 that depended on the domain (Kamsties et al. 2001, section 4). The ambiguities that change actions are mostly not the ones a linguist would list.

## 2. A reader gets one reading and does not feel the other

**Both meanings are active for a moment, and one is chosen without awareness.** Swinney (1979), *JVLVB* 18:645–659 (abstract): without strongly biasing context, all meanings of an ambiguous word are accessed briefly, but are "not available to conscious introspection". Rodd (2020), "Settling into semantic space", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 15:411–427 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619885860), original author manuscript), reviews forty years of this work. All familiar meanings become available, at speeds set by how common each meaning is and by prior context. Which meaning arrives first depends on the reader's recent history: one encounter with a less common meaning boosts it for 20 to 40 minutes, and up to a day with sleep. *Standing:* narrowed from strict "exhaustive access"; the reordered-access account holds. *Population:* people. My inference: a writer who has spent a session using a word in a project-specific sense is primed toward that sense, and a fresh reader is not.

**The second reading shapes behavior without becoming conscious.** MacKay (1966), *Perception & Psychophysics* 1:426–436 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03215819), original). People completed sentence fragments, two-thirds of them ambiguous with both meanings about equally likely. Only 3 of about 43 noticed any ambiguity during a trial. Yet ambiguous fragments took longer and produced more stuttering (45 against 21 trials), more off-topic completions (44 against 17) and more ungrammatical ones (52 against 14). One participant laughed on 20 ambiguous trials without knowing it. *Standing:* classic; narrowed by Bever, Garrett & Hurtig (1973, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198109), abstract): both meanings are processed while the clause is incomplete, and only one is kept once it ends. Readers with more working memory keep both structures of a temporary ambiguity for several words, and readers with less drop to the likelier one (MacDonald, Just & Carpenter 1992, *Cognitive Psychology* 24:56–98, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(92)90003-K), abstract).

**Readers keep a first reading even after the sentence rules it out, and feel sure of it.** Christianson, Hollingworth, Halliwell & Ferreira (2001), *Cognitive Psychology* 42:368–407 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.2001.0752), original). After "While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods", 47.4% answered yes to "Did the man hunt the deer?" when the ambiguous region was long, with mean confidence 3.41 on a 4-point scale. Reanalysis "might not be completed unless questions probing unresolved aspects ... challenge" the interpretation (Christianson et al. 2006, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326950dp4202_6), abstract). Readers do build the right structure and fail to discard the first (Slattery et al. 2013, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2013.04.001), original). *Standing:* replicated as an effect, contested as a theory. A 2024 review of the roughly 20 direct tests found the best support for two claims: ambiguities often stay unresolved, and the depth of processing depends on the task. It called "good-enough processing" as a theory too vague to falsify (Frances 2024, *Frontiers in Psychology* 15:1323700, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1323700), original). *Population:* people.

**Depth is set by what the reader expects to be asked.** Swets, Desmet, Clifton & Ferreira (2008), *Memory & Cognition* 36:201–216 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.1.201), original). Ambiguous sentences were read faster than disambiguated ones only when readers expected shallow questions. Readers who expected questions about the ambiguous part read more carefully, and even then answered more slowly for ambiguous sentences, as if they had never settled them. A German study found ambiguous sentences read more slowly when the task required resolving them (Logačev & Vasishth 2016, *Cognitive Science* 40:266–298, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12228), abstract). *Standing:* the mechanism is contested; task dependence is supported. *Population:* people.

**The reader repairs the input toward what it expects.** Gibson, Bergen & Piantadosi (2013), *PNAS* 110:8051–8056 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216438110), original). Readers treat input as noisy and infer the plausible intended sentence. An implausible sentence one word away from a plausible one was read literally 56.1% of the time, and 93.4% when the plausible version was two edits away. Literal readings fell further when the context looked error-prone and rose when implausible sentences were common. Readers adapt to the kinds of error in their environment (Ryskin et al. 2018, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.08.018), abstract). *Standing:* replicated within the paper and extended. *Population:* people. **What it explains:** a reading the reader did not expect gets repaired into one it did. That is why a confident misreading feels like reading, and why a writer's slip often passes unnoticed.

**A good fit hides detail.** Readers often leave word meanings underspecified. A change to a word was noticed more often when the word was in linguistic focus, set up by a cleft sentence or a prior question that raised alternatives (Sturt, Sanford, Stewart & Dawydiak 2004, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 11:882–888, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03196716), original; Sanford & Sturt 2002, abstract). *Population:* people.

**Listeners and viewers miss the alternative too.** With an ambiguous instruction and a display supporting both readings, eye tracking showed addressees did not consider the unintended object 40% of the time (Kraljic & Brennan 2005, *Cognitive Psychology* 50:194–231, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2004.08.002), original). Outside language, viewers not told that a figure was reversible (the vase and faces) looked for up to three minutes, and about half never saw it reverse; once told, "virtually all subjects reversed quickly and frequently" (Girgus, Rock & Egatz 1977, *Perception & Psychophysics* 22:550–556, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198762), original). About a third reversed uninformed in a replication (Rock & Mitchener 1992, abstract). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people. It is the cleanest case of a second reading becoming available once a person knows it exists.

**Each person is consistent, and people differ from each other.** Budescu, Por & Broomell (2012), "Effective communication of uncertainty in the IPCC reports", *Climatic Change* 113:181–200 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-011-0330-3), original), reviewing decades of work on probability words: variability between individuals is "very high and ... considerably larger than the variance observed within-individuals." People read "likely" the same way each time and differently from one another. Twenty participants produced 111 distinct phrases for 11 probabilities (Budescu et al. 1988, secondary via Budescu 2012). Weather forecasters trained on fixed meanings reverted to everyday meanings outside forecasting: it is "extremely difficult to 'legislate meaning'" (Wallsten et al. 1986, secondary). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people. **What it explains:** if each reader is self-consistent, nothing inside one reader signals ambiguity. This is why introspection fails and a second reader is needed.

**The feeling of understanding cannot tell a wrong reading from a right one.** Baker & Brown (1980), *Metacognitive Skills and Reading*, Center for the Study of Reading Technical Report 188 (ERIC ED195932, original), a review. One way comprehension fails is that the reader builds a consistent interpretation that is not the author's. "Readers who understand incorrectly have much the same feelings as readers who understand correctly," so they "can hardly be expected to take remedial action." Readers who cannot build any coherent reading are the ones who seek help. *Population:* people. **What it explains:** every reading of an ambiguous message is a consistent interpretation, so ambiguity is the case the feeling of understanding is worst at.

**Even readers told to search miss contradictions.** Glenberg, Wilkinson & Epstein (1982), "The illusion of knowing", *Memory & Cognition* 10:597–602 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03202442), original). Adults explicitly told to look for contradictions between adjacent sentences missed up to 51% of them. The chance of missing a contradiction while rating one's comprehension high was .106 for one-paragraph texts and .372 for three-paragraph texts. The authors call the results "apparently incompatible with the notion that readers engage in active and accurate on-line monitoring." Children aged 8 to 12 were equally unlikely at every age to report contradictions in short essays, and a warning that contradictions were present helped mainly the oldest (Markman 1979, *Child Development* 50:643–655, abstract plus Baker & Brown). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people.

**The second representation is usually never formed.** Contradictions went unnoticed more often when spotting them required an inference, when the two sentences were in different paragraphs, and when paraphrase carried the same concepts. A contradiction is caught only if both propositions are active together (Epstein, Glenberg & Bradley 1984, *Memory & Cognition* 12:355–360, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198295), original). In children aged 6 to 11, failures went with incomplete recall of one of the two propositions, not with failing to compare two that were both remembered (Vosniadou, Pearson & Rogers 1988, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 80:27–39, original report version). About 40% of high-school readers detected a contradiction between sentences 2 and 6 of a six-sentence text. Those who missed it recalled only one of the two sentences or explained the contradiction away, and a simulation reproduced this as one proposition being suppressed by the reader's overall interpretation (Otero & Kintsch 1992, *Psychological Science* 3:229–235, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00034.x), secondary via search summaries of the abstract). *Standing:* consistent across labs. *Population:* people. **What it explains:** Markman's inference effect, Glenberg's distance effect, and the semantic illusions below. For ambiguity, the competing reading is the second representation that is never formed.

**A good fit hides the error.** Asked "When an airplane crashes, where should the survivors be buried?", readers caught the problem 23–30% of the time. With a bicycle-crash scenario, which fits "survivors" less well, they caught it 73–80% of the time (Barton & Sanford 1993, *Memory & Cognition* 21:477–487, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03197179), original). In the Moses illusion ("How many animals of each kind did Moses take on the Ark?"), most people answer "two". *Standing:* replicated many times. In a total sample of 914, the illusion persisted with multiple-choice answers, and money for catching it helped only a little (Speckmann & Unkelbach 2020, *Memory & Cognition*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-020-01128-z), abstract). Undetected errors later entered people's answers, and prior knowledge did not protect them (Bottoms, Eslick & Marsh 2010, abstract). Resisting a related impostor word tracked skill at using long-term knowledge, and resisting the pull of context tracked working memory (Hannon & Daneman 2001, abstract). *Population:* people.

**Judgments of understanding run on ease, unless a task forces retrieval.** Generating keywords or a summary improved the accuracy of people's judgments of their own comprehension, but only when done after a delay, because delayed generation gives "necessary diagnostic cues about comprehension" (Thiede, Dunlosky, Griffin & Wiley 2005, *JEP: LMC*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.6.1267), abstract). People rated their understanding of how things work lower after trying to explain the mechanism. The illusion of explanatory depth (Rozenblit & Keil 2002, *Cognitive Science* 26:521–562, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1), abstract) replicated in three preregistered studies, though a downstream effect on attitudes did not (Crawford & Ruscio 2021, *Psychological Science*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620972367), abstract). *Standing:* the drop in rated understanding is replicated. *Population:* people. "Noticed" and "acted better" are separate outcomes: here the first replicated and the second did not.

## 3. A writer cannot see the ambiguity in its own words

**Speakers rarely avoid ambiguity, and the reason is how production works.** V. S. Ferreira (2008), "Ambiguity, accessibility, and a division of labor for communicative success", *Psychology of Learning and Motivation* 49:209–246 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-7421(08)00006-6), original author manuscript), reports two lines of experiments.

- Speakers used the optional "that" ("the coach knew *that* you...") only 2.3% more when leaving it out would make the sentence ambiguous, not a significant difference, and 0.3% less for a severe garden path. "That" tracked how easy the next words were to produce, not ambiguity (Ferreira & Dell 2000, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.1999.0730)).
- Speakers named a target, such as a flying bat, next to a foil sharing its name, a baseball bat. Without a preview they avoided the bare word "bat" only 11% more often than in control displays. When the foil was just a bigger bat of the same kind, they "almost always" avoided the bare word. A preview helped somewhat. Speakers caught the problem mainly when about to use the same word for the second object too: "swan, bat, and baseball bat" (Ferreira, Slevc & Rogers 2005, *Cognition* 96:263–284, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2004.09.002), chapter account plus abstract).

Ferreira's explanation is the deeper layer. Detecting linguistic ambiguity needs a "production U-turn": the speaker would have to take a candidate wording and check it against "an open-ended set of unrelated and unintended alternative meanings". Production runs only forward from the intended meaning. A nonlinguistic ambiguity (two bats of the same kind) is visible in the scene. A linguistic one exists only in a meaning the speaker is not thinking of. *Standing:* replicated across Ferreira's studies, and Wasow's review agrees. Speakers in dialogue disambiguated somewhat more when the scene was ambiguous, a smaller effect than echoing the partner's wording (Haywood, Pickering & Branigan 2005, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2005.01541.x), abstract with numbers via Wasow). *Population:* people.

**Why self-monitoring misses it.** Speech-monitoring models compare what was said against the speaker's own standard: the intended message, word, or a forward model of it. "No model has an adequate account of error detection in other-perception" (Lind & Hartsuiker 2020, *Journal of Cognition* 3(1):16, [doi](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.118), original editorial). My inference: a check against your own intended meaning cannot flag an unintended reading, because nothing in the check represents the reader's alternatives.

**Speakers mark ambiguity when they know it is there.** Neither untrained nor professional speakers reliably used disambiguating intonation when reading ambiguous sentences in context. Speakers made aware of the ambiguity and told to convey one meaning did (Allbritton, McKoon & Ratcliff 1996, *JEP: LMC* 22:714–735, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.22.3.714), abstract). Speakers used effective intonation mainly when the scene supported both readings (Snedeker & Trueswell 2003, abstract and secondary). Kraljic & Brennan (2005, original) found speakers marked boundaries whether or not the situation was ambiguous, apparently for themselves. Speakers who had just been the listener did no better at marking specific ambiguities: "experience with a general potential for ambiguity may not bring speakers any closer" to detecting a specific one. *Standing:* contested on intonation, consistent on awareness. *Population:* people.

**Knowing what you meant hides the other reading.** Bartlett (1982), "Learning to revise: some component processes", in Nystrand (ed.), *What Writers Know*, pp. 345–363 (original, reproduced in [ERIC ED213042](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED213042.pdf)). Fifth graders whose own stories had an ambiguous reference (who "he" was) detected it when revising 17% of the time (5 of 30). Children whose stories had syntax problems detected those 53% of the time. A week later, editing other children's texts, 73% caught at least one ambiguous reference, 57% of all such problems. The children were willing to change their drafts: 57% made some change. Bartlett's account: revising your own text requires you to "inhibit interpretations based on knowledge of the writer's own intentions". She predicted the gap would be largest for problems that such knowledge can mask, and it was. Skill with others' texts "need not be accompanied by skill in detecting similar problems in one's own." *Standing:* single study, consistent with Hayes et al. (1985, ERIC ED267396, abstract): writers find faulty references harder to detect in their own text. *Population:* people.

The same mechanism shows up earlier and later in life.

- First graders who knew which object a speaker meant often said an ambiguous message "could not refer to" the object the speaker had not meant. Uninformed first graders detected the ambiguity (Beal & Flavell 1984, *Child Development* 55:920–928, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1984.tb03829.x), abstract).
- Adults who knew a speaker meant a remark sarcastically attributed that reading to an addressee who could not know it, even for written messages (Keysar 1994, "The illusory transparency of intention", *Cognitive Psychology* 26:165–208, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.1994.1006), abstract). Replicated online and offline, and partly reduced when the addressee was in narrative focus (Weingartner & Klin 2005, *Memory & Cognition* 33:48–58, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03195296), original). This is the comprehension-side mechanism behind the earlier note's Keysar & Henly result.
- People found fewer errors in essays they had written than in unfamiliar essays, but more in familiar essays by others than in unfamiliar ones. The own-text disadvantage was a by-product of extreme familiarity (Daneman & Stainton 1993, *Reading and Writing* 5:297–313, abstract).
- Knowing a technical term led people to overestimate substantially how many others knew it (Hayes & Bajzek 2008, *Written Communication* 25:104–118, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0741088307311209), abstract; a feedback tutor was tested, effect size not in the abstract).

**The hearer of a read-back hears what it meant.** In 417 NASA incident reports of misheard numbers, pilots "heard what they expected to hear, heard what they wanted to hear and frequently did not hear what they did not anticipate hearing." Controllers often failed to catch their own slips when the pilot read them back (Monan 1986, *Human factors in aviation operations: The hearback problem*, NASA CR-177398, [PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19890007398/downloads/19890007398.pdf), original). My reading: the controller compared the read-back with what it meant, not with what it said. *Population:* people.

## 4. Judging "is this clear?" consults the reading you already have

**Preference makes a text look clear, and asking about other readers removes the bias.** Farnsworth, Guzior & Malani (2010), "Ambiguity about ambiguity: An empirical inquiry into legal interpretation", *Journal of Legal Analysis* 2(1):257–300 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/jla/2.1.257), original working paper). About 900 to 1,000 law students at three schools rated 11 real federal criminal statutes, each randomly asked one of three questions: is it ambiguous, are both readings plausible, or would ordinary readers of English disagree. All also gave a policy preference.

- On the first two questions, the more strongly people preferred an outcome, the less ambiguous they said the statute was (p < .001). On the question about ordinary readers, preference had no effect.
- Against real disagreement: on one statute, readers actually split 55/45 on the better reading. Asked whether it was ambiguous, 55% of those with mild preferences said yes and 15% of those with strong preferences. None of the strongly pro-government respondents saw any ambiguity.
- The question about other readers was not accurate either. On a statute where readers split 80/20, 74% predicted disagreement. It removed the bias and overestimated ambiguity where readers mostly agreed.
- A year of law school made no difference.

The authors' account: the internal question becomes an inquiry into "how strongly they themselves feel sure", easily contaminated by preference. The external question forces respondents "to look outside themselves". *Standing:* single large study, consistent across 11 statutes. *Population:* people. *Prompted:* yes, by reframing the question.

**Your reading feels like the normal reading.** Lay readers given insurance scenarios on which courts had split each believed "that their interpretation is the normal interpretation, even when it is not", whichever party it favored. Judges showed the same bias (Solan, Rosenblatt & Osherson 2008, "False consensus bias in contract interpretation", *Columbia Law Review* 108(5):1268–1300, abstract). In laypeople and judges, the larger the majority a person was in, the less difficult they rated the case (Sheppard, Coenen & Moshirnia 2025, *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/jels.70017), abstract). The feeling that a case is hard signals being in the minority, not that the text is ambiguous. *Standing:* single studies. *Population:* people, including judges. Readers of probability words in the climate reports assigned higher numbers when they believed in climate change (Budescu et al. 2012, original): motivated reading reaches numbers too.

**Asking people directly is slow and unreliable.** People judging whether an instruction matched more than one object in a display took about 4.5 seconds per correct judgment and were at chance (53.9%) on the hardest cases. They used duplicate objects in the display as a shortcut for "ambiguous" (Kraljic & Brennan 2005, Experiment 2, original). In requirements reviews, "simply asking whether there is an ambiguity is not much help" (Kamsties, Berry & Paech 2001, section 5).

**A settled reading crowds out the alternatives.** People predicting behavior in loosely described situations gave confidence ranges no wider when they could not assume their reading of the situation was right. "Only in conditions where Ss were obliged to offer alternative construals did they appropriately broaden their confidence limits" (Griffin, Dunning & Ross 1990, *JPSP* 59:1128–1139, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.59.6.1128), abstract). *Standing:* one paper, four studies. *Population:* people. This sits beside the earlier note's Eyal et al. (2018): imagining another's perspective did not help, while generating the alternative did. Section 6 collects the debiasing studies that build on this.

## 5. What makes the second reading visible

The methods that work across fields have one thing in common: they put a second reading next to the first.

**A second reader, compared with the first.** Gause & Weinberg (1989), *Exploring Requirements*, as quoted by Kamsties et al.: "Perhaps, the most effective approach is to hand requirements to several different stakeholders, to ask each for an interpretation, and to compare these interpretations afterwards" (secondary). Kamsties adds that it is "feasible only for small sets".

Spoletini, Ferrari, Bano, Zowghi & Gnesi (2018), "Interview review", REFSQ 2018, LNCS 10753:101–118 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-77243-1_7), original). In 42 role-played requirements interviews, the analyst noticed during the live interview 32% of all ambiguities eventually found. Reviews of the recording by the same analyst and by an outside reviewer found the other 68%. The live analyst and the outside reviewer overlapped on 2% of the total. The outside reviewer found 37% that nobody else did. The review prompt asked the reviewer to stop wherever they would ask "Do you mean A or B?" or "I thought that with X you meant Y, was I wrong?". *Standing:* single controlled study. *Population:* people (students). **What it shows:** detection by any one person is low-recall, and different readers find different ambiguities. Live interaction suppressed noticing.

Survey pretesting points the same way. Three organizations applying three pretesting methods to the same questionnaires agreed only weakly on which questions had problems (rank correlation .27 between expert review and cognitive interviews; kappa about .3 between organizations). Vague questions and vague terms made up 31% of the problems found (Rothgeb, Willis & Forsyth 2005, Census RSM 2005-02, [PDF](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2005/adrm/rsm2005-02.pdf), original). Expert panels found the most problems in an earlier comparison (Presser & Blair 1994, *Sociological Methodology* 24:73–104, secondary via Willis et al. 1999).

**Forcing the words into a form with no room for vagueness.** Kent (1964), "Words of Estimative Probability", *Studies in Intelligence* ([PDF](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Words-of-Estimative-Probability.pdf), original). A 1951 estimate called a Soviet attack on Yugoslavia "a serious possibility". A policy reader asked what odds that meant. Kent had meant about 65:35, and the reader had read it much lower. Kent then asked the board that approved the wording: "the low man was thinking of about 20 to 80, the high of 80 to 20." He was shaken more by the realization that the board "had failed to communicate with each other" than by its failure with its readers. *Standing:* historical case; its mechanism is replicated by the work on probability words. *Population:* people. **What it shows:** agreeing on wording is not agreeing on meaning, and the gap surfaced only when someone forced a translation into numbers.

Kamsties, Berry & Paech (2001), "Detecting ambiguities in requirements documents using inspections", WISE'01, pp. 68–80 ([PDF](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~dberry/FTP_SITE/tech.reports/kamsties.berry.paech.pdf), original). Building a formal model of the requirements did not reveal ambiguity. It removed it "by causing, often unconscious, cho[o]sing of one interpretation", turning an ambiguous requirement into "an unambiguously wrong formal" one. Their example requirement, shut off the pumps if the water level "remains above 100 meters for more than 4 seconds", has four readings (mean, median, root mean square, minimum), and the engineers "did not notice this ambiguity and quietly assumed the fourth." Their fix was to turn the modeler's choice points into explicit questions. With teams of three reviewers working 4.5 hours in total, a generic technique found 18% of the ambiguities, one tailored to the domain 25%, and building a model 9%. *Standing:* one research program; the full experiments are in Kamsties' dissertation, not read. *Population:* people.

**The alternative meaning must be in the reader's path.** In a representative US sample of 556, readers matched the climate reports' intended meaning of probability words 20.8% of the time with no aid, 18.8% with the reports' translation table, and 30.1% with the numbers printed next to the words (Budescu et al. 2012, original). Readers deviated from the guidelines "even when the respondents had access to these guidelines" (Budescu, Broomell & Por 2009, *Psychological Science* 20(3):299–308, abstract). With intelligence probability terms, 924 participants matched the standard 66% of the time with numbers bracketed in the text and 32% without; click-through tables and tooltips did not substantially help (Wintle et al. 2019, *PLoS ONE* 14(4):e0213522, abstract). *Standing:* replicated across two domains. *Population:* people. My inference, from section 2: a reader who does not experience ambiguity has no reason to look anything up, so an aid the reader must choose to consult fails.

**Read-back catches transmission errors. Paraphrase tests meaning.**

- An FAA review of voice-tape studies found read-back errors in less than 1% of transmissions, most caught. Errors rose with the number of items per message: messages with five or more items were 4% of transmissions and 26% of errors (Schroeder, Bailey, Pounds & Manning 2006, DOT/FAA/AM-06/21, [PDF](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200621.pdf), original, summarizing Cardosi's studies, secondary for those).
- Pilots treated controller silence after a read-back as confirmation, and this "'blank check' reliance upon the hearback link" led to accepted descents below terrain (Monan 1986, original). A check meant as redundancy became a dependency.
- Read-back of critical lab results caught errors in 3.5% of 822 calls and added 12.8 seconds per call (Barenfanger et al. 2004, *Am J Clin Pathol* 121:801–803, abstract).
- Physicians checked patients' understanding for 12% of new concepts. Checking was associated with better blood-sugar control (adjusted odds ratio 15.15, wide interval) (Schillinger et al. 2003, *Arch Intern Med* 163:83–90, abstract; observational). A systematic review found teach-back, in which the reader restates in its own words, effective in 19 of 20 studies (Talevski et al. 2020, *PLoS ONE* 15(4):e0231350, abstract).

My synthesis: read-back repeats the words, so it can confirm a shared word with two meanings. A paraphrase forces the reader's meaning into new words, and that is where a different reading shows. It is the same move as Kent's odds and the analyst's summary in Spoletini et al. This bounds the earlier note's I-PASS result: the receiver's "synthesis" step is the part that tests meaning.

**Knowing that an alternative exists makes it findable.** Told that every sentence was ambiguous, students found the second meaning in a median of roughly 4 to 7 seconds, and missed it within 90 seconds on 15 of 1,585 trials. It took longest when one meaning was strongly favored (MacKay & Bever 1967, *Perception & Psychophysics* 2:193–200, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03213049), original). Participants not told the items were ambiguous, given 7 seconds, missed many, and found it "quite difficult" to paraphrase the two meanings distinctly (Prideaux & Baker 1976, *Human Communication* 1(1), [PDF](https://cjslpa.ca/files/1976_HumComm_Vol_01/No_01_Spring_1-84/Prideaux_Baker_HumComm_1976.pdf), original). *Population:* people. That last finding bears on Manuel's step of formulating "the difference between the two concepts in words": stating the difference is itself hard, even once both readings are in mind.

**Using the reading exposes a gap. Acting on one reading can hide an ambiguity.** Two findings look opposed, and the difference between them matters for Manuel's test.

- First and third graders heard instructions for a card game or a magic trick with a crucial step left out. Third graders noticed far more readily. First graders often noticed only when they tried to carry out the instructions (Markman 1977, "Realizing that you don't understand", *Child Development* 48:986–992, abstract plus Baker & Brown). Kindergartners building from taped instructions showed puzzlement while doing the task, such as replaying the tape, even when they later said the message was fine (Flavell, Speer, Green & August 1981, *Monographs of the SRCD* 46(5), [doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/1165875), abstract). Explaining or self-explaining a text has the same effect in older readers (Rozenblit & Keil; Chi et al. 1994, abstract). And readers expecting questions that depend on the ambiguous part read it more deeply (Swets et al. 2008).
- Five- to nine-year-olds responded differently to ambiguous and clear messages only when they were *prevented* from pointing at a candidate object (Robinson & Whittaker 1985, *Developmental Psychology* 21(3):446, abstract). Otherwise children relied on what was salient, then guessed instead of asking (Speer et al. 1983, abstract).

My reconciliation: carrying out an instruction that is *missing* something fails, and the failure is the signal. Carrying out an *ambiguous* instruction succeeds under whichever reading was chosen, so nothing fails and nothing signals. Acting on one reading is not Manuel's test. His test asks what each reading would lead to, which needs both readings to be produced first. ClarifyGPT (section 8) does exactly that: it executes several readings and compares the results.

**A message on paper is easier to judge than one in the air.** First graders judged whether a message picked out one object better when it was written as well as spoken (Bonitatibus & Flavell 1985, *Developmental Psychology*, abstract). Making the message an object to inspect helped. *Single study.* *Population:* people.

## 6. Can noticing be learned or prompted?

**Urging people to look harder moves their threshold, not their accuracy.** Kamas, Reder & Ayers (1996), *Memory & Cognition* 24:687–699 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03201094), original), tried four ways to make people catch Moses-illusion distortions, including capitalizing the distorted word and asking them to monitor only for distortions. Each raised reports of distortions, but mostly by making people more willing to say "distorted". They also called more undistorted questions distorted, and even with capitals they missed the distortion at least one time in four. The authors failed to replicate earlier claims that focusing attention improves accuracy. A hard-to-read font cut answering "two" from 88% to 53%, and cut correct answers to an undistorted question by the same amount (Song & Schwarz 2008, *Social Cognition* 26:791–799, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1521/soco.2008.26.6.791), original; tiny samples, and the wider literature on hard-to-read fonts has replication problems). Readers explicitly told to search for contradictions still missed up to half (Glenberg et al. 1982). Telling people to "consider its completeness" raised their estimate of what a fault tree left out by about half, still far short of the truth (Fischhoff et al. 1978). *Standing:* consistent across these studies. *Population:* people. This is the human counterpart of section 8's finding that prompting a model to consider ambiguity raises its asking everywhere without improving where it asks.

**A specific standard or a supplied alternative works.**

- Children told what kind of problem to look for found more of that kind (Markman & Gorin 1981, secondary). Children aged 5 to 11 told to find three named kinds of mistake did "considerably better than that reported in non-instructed settings". Internal inconsistency was the hardest standard to apply, and 5-year-olds rose from 25% to 58% after being told they had missed something. Adults told to look for all three kinds found a mean of 94% (Baker 1984, *JECP* 38:289–311, original 1983 report version). **What it explains:** monitoring is not one skill. A reader finds only what its active standard can see.
- Listing a reason that *contradicts* one's answer improved calibration, and listing a supporting reason did not (Koriat, Lichtenstein & Fischhoff 1980, *JEP: HLM* 6:107–118, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.6.2.107), original abstract and introduction). Generating reasons against an anchor reduced anchoring, in experts too (Mussweiler, Strack & Pfeiffer 2000, abstract).
- Explaining *any* plausible alternative outcome, not only the opposite, debiased judgments, because it set off spontaneous consideration of further alternatives (Hirt & Markman 1995, *JPSP* 69:1069–1086, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.69.6.1069), abstract).
- Asking for many alternatives can backfire. Listing 2 alternatives to a known outcome removed hindsight bias. Listing 10 felt hard, and the difficulty made people conclude there were few alternatives, which increased the bias (Sanna, Schwarz & Stocker 2002, *JEP: LMC*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.28.3.497), abstract).
- Timing decides it. 297 family doctors diagnosed nine cases. A list of diagnoses to consider, shown *before* they gathered information, raised accuracy from .63 to .69 without lengthening their search. The same list shown *after* a first diagnosis did not help (Kostopoulou et al. 2015, *British Journal of General Practice*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp15X683161), original). Replicated with 150 doctors in Greece, pooled odds ratio 1.40; doctors given the late list "rarely changed their initial diagnoses" (Kostopoulou et al. 2015b, *Family Practice*, abstract). The paper reports that in a naturalistic trial junior doctors consulted a diagnostic aid about 2% of the time. People do not know when advice would help, which argues for aids triggered by the situation over aids one must decide to use.
- Accountability *before* a decision, to an audience whose views are unknown, produces "preemptive self-criticism", in which people consider several perspectives and anticipate objections. Accountability *after* produces "defensive bolstering". It has no effect "if, even after increased attention to one's decision process, no new ways of solving the problem come into awareness" (Lerner & Tetlock 1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125:255–275, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.2.255), original).
- A gap does not produce a question by itself. Learners must "know enough to know what is not known": trained learners asked more questions on hard material, novices on easy material (Miyake & Norman 1979, *JVLVB* 18:357–364, abstract).

*Standing:* the timing result and the consider-the-opposite family are replicated; the backfire is a single study. *Population:* people.

**Clinical reflection shows the same pattern.** Structured reflection, in which doctors list alternative diagnoses and weigh findings for and against each, helped residents on complex cases and not simple ones (Mamede, Schmidt & Penaforte 2008, *Medical Education*, abstract). It also corrected errors induced by recently seeing similar cases (Mamede et al. 2010, *JAMA* 304:1198–1203, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.1276), abstract). Telling residents to be "careful, thorough, reflective" left scores unchanged, 45.0% against 44.5% for a cohort told to be quick (Norman et al. 2014, *Academic Medicine* 89:277–284, abstract; cohorts from different years, not randomized). Given the chance to revise, residents changed 8% of diagnoses, and scores barely moved (Monteiro et al. 2015, abstract). Medical students told to generate 2 or 6 alternatives did no better than their first impressions (Lambe, Hevey & Kelly 2018, abstract): the effect failed to extend to novices. Reviews put guided reflection at an effect size of about 0.38 (Prakash et al. 2019, abstract) and workplace cognitive tools at 0.20 (Staal et al. 2022, abstract). Strategies aimed at recognizing one's biases were ineffective, and strategies that reorganize knowledge had small, consistent benefits (Norman et al. 2017, abstract). *Standing:* replicated in direction, small in size. *Population:* people. **Reading across these:** what works names alternatives and weighs them, and only when the case is complex, the knowledge exists, and the alternative arrives early.

**Why a partial set feels complete.** People shown a fault tree for "car won't start", with major branches removed, should have raised the "all other problems" branch from .078 to .468. They roughly doubled it. Experienced mechanics did no better, and years of experience did not predict noticing what was missing (Fischhoff, Slovic & Lichtenstein 1978, *JEP: HPP* 4:330–344, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.4.2.330), original). Professional managers showed the same effect, and having them extend the tree by generating extra causes worked as a corrective (Dube-Rioux & Russo 1988, abstract). Experts and novices generating causes for car faults each produced about 3.4 per problem, roughly 19% of the set the group generated together. They rated their own sets as fairly complete: pooled across people, the probabilities summed to an adjusted 504% (Mehle 1982, *Acta Psychologica*, original dissertation version; small sample). The account that explains these is HyGene (Thomas, Dougherty, Sprenger & Harbison 2008, *Psychological Review* 115:155–185, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.115.1.155), abstract plus Sprenger et al. 2011, original). Data in the environment cue hypotheses from memory. Working memory caps how many are held. Judgment compares the favored hypothesis only against the alternatives actually generated. Dividing attention while learning cut the number generated later, and that number fully explained the effect on judgment. *Standing:* replicated effect, with a tested model. *Population:* people. **What it explains:** a reader that has generated one reading has normalized its confidence over a set of one. An alternative reading never generated counts for nothing.

**Seizing and freezing.** Kruglanski & Webster (1996), "Motivated closing of the mind: 'seizing' and 'freezing'", *Psychological Review* 103:263–283 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.103.2.263), original). The need for closure has two tendencies: urgency, which seizes on early information, and permanence, which freezes on it. It rises with time pressure, noise, fatigue and dull tasks, and falls with fear of being wrong. People high in it generated the fewest hypotheses about what an object was and were the most confident (Mayseless & Kruglanski 1987, as summarized in the review). They also resisted persuasion when they already had a view, and were more open to it when they did not: they seize whatever comes first (Kruglanski, Webster & Klem 1993, abstract). In 100 diagnostic errors, premature closure, "failure to continue considering reasonable alternatives after an initial diagnosis was reached", was the most common cause, and lack of knowledge was uncommon (Graber, Franklin & Gordon 2005, *Archives of Internal Medicine*, abstract). *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people. Whether a model has anything like the need for closure is untested. The nearest model result is Laban et al.'s finding (section 7) that early commitment goes with failure.

**How children learn that a message can be ambiguous.**

- When a message fit more than one card, 5-year-olds blamed the listener, and children of 7 and older blamed the speaker. The younger children did blame the speaker when the message pointed clearly at the wrong card, so their failure was specific to ambiguity (Robinson & Robinson 1977, *Developmental Psychology* 13:156–161, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.13.2.156), secondary via Asher & Wigfield's 1980 review, which was read in the original).
- Development includes learning that "an ambiguous message is intrinsically unclear and remains a poor message regardless of the listener's response to it". Younger children judged a message by whether it happened to work (Singer & Flavell 1981, *Child Development*, abstract).
- Five- and six-year-olds treat a message's meaning as the intended outcome, not as "a clue from which it may or may not be possible to infer the intended meaning". The authors propose that schooling teaches the clue view (Robinson & Whittaker 1987, *British Journal of Developmental Psychology*, abstract).
- Five- and six-year-olds overestimated what they knew after ambiguous messages and, unlike older children, did not seek more information. Their interpretations were "not tentative at the outset" (Beck & Robinson 2001, *JECP*, abstract).
- Children aged 4 to 8 marked both possible doors when an outcome was not yet decided, but only one door when a block was already hidden behind one of them and they just did not know which (Robinson, Rowley, Beck, Carroll & Apperly 2006, *Child Development*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2006.00964.x), abstract). An ambiguous message is the second kind of uncertainty: the speaker meant one thing, and the listener does not know which. That is the kind under which children collapse to a single guess. Whether adults show the same asymmetry was not checked.
- Holding several possibilities at once is a distinct ability that develops later than imagining one alternative (Beck et al. 2006, abstract). Younger children who said a text was ambiguous were still less likely to name the alternative reading (Bonitatibus & Beal 1992, original report).

*Standing:* a consistent developmental literature, mostly read as abstracts. *Population:* people (children). **What it explains:** the shift from "the message is the meaning" to "the message is a clue" is what makes ambiguity noticeable at all. The epistemic-uncertainty finding suggests why "the writer meant one thing" pushes a reader toward a single guess.

**Training children to notice and ask.**

- 94 children taught an action rule, ask for more information when a message is ambiguous, asked more questions after ambiguous messages and chose the right object more often. The effect lasted two weeks. A rule to compare the message against each candidate object had no effect (Patterson, Massad & Cosgrove 1978, *Developmental Psychology*, abstract).
- Telling a child explicitly whether the listener had understood worked much better immediately after the message than at the end of the exchange. Imposing the same behavior, such as asking, without the explanation worked almost as well (Robinson & Robinson 1985, *IJBD* 8:285–299, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/016502548500800304), abstract).
- Comparison training improved accuracy and lasted, but did not generalize to other referential tasks, even when trained on several (Asher & Wigfield 1980 review, ERIC ED191014, original). Training as a speaker did not transfer to listening (Sonnenschein & Whitehurst 1983, abstract).

*Standing:* real and durable, but narrow. *Population:* people (children).

**Learning to state both meanings.** Detecting lexical ambiguity develops in first grade and predicts reading a year later (Cairns, Waltzman & Schlisselberg 2004, abstract). Kindergartners can be trained to detect ambiguous sentences. But "knowledge of both meanings of a homophone is not sufficient to report both meanings of a sentence that contains that homophone": detection also needs "an ability to think flexibly about language" (Kamowski-Shakibai & Cairns 2016, *Journal of Child Language*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/S030500091500015X), abstract). In a randomized trial with 46 third graders, four sessions on homonyms, ambiguous sentences and riddles improved ambiguity detection, transferred to detecting inconsistencies in stories, and improved reading comprehension on one measure (d = .73) but not another (Zipke, Ehri & Cairns 2009, *Reading Research Quarterly* 44:300–321, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1598/RRQ.44.3.4), original). Children aged 7 to 9 who discussed joking riddles in pairs gained more in comprehension than controls. The gains went with more talk about language, "in particular with the simultaneous explanation of two meanings" (Yuill 2009, *First Language*, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0142723708097561), abstract). *Standing:* small trials that agree with each other. *Population:* people (children). This is the nearest measured evidence for Manuel's step of formulating "the difference between the two concepts in words": saying both meanings side by side predicted improvement.

**Debiasing can be trained, with some transfer.** A single debiasing game cut several biases, including confirmation bias, by at least 32% immediately and 24% two months later, and held in contexts not taught (Morewedge et al. 2015, abstract). Trained graduate students were 19% less likely to choose the hypothesis-confirming solution in a surprise field case (Sellier, Scopelliti & Morewedge 2019, *Psychological Science*, abstract). *Standing:* a single research group. *Population:* people. These are the strongest transfer results found, and they come from training with feedback, not from instruction alone.

**From the applied fields and the writing research.**

- **Procedures that produce the alternative work. Exhortation does not.** Reframing to other readers (Farnsworth), "Do you mean A or B?" prompts (Spoletini), alternatives printed in the reader's path (Budescu; Wintle), forced translation into numbers, choice points or test cases (Kent; Kamsties), paraphrase (teach-back), being told the alternative exists (MacKay & Bever; Girgus), and being obliged to offer alternative construals (Griffin et al.). What failed: asking "is it ambiguous?", optional glossaries and tooltips, bare repetition.
- **Changing the task definition taps skills writers already have.** Eight minutes of instruction on revising for meaning rather than surface produced more global revision and better texts across the treated group (Wallace & Hayes 1991, *Research in the Teaching of English*, ERIC ED324691, original): "The change in task definition allowed students to tap revision skills that they already had available." Readers who expected questions about the ambiguous part read it more deeply (Swets et al. 2008).
- **Reading as the reader helps writers.** A "reading-as-the-reader" condition improved fifth and ninth graders' descriptions (Holliway 2004, abstract). Watching peers try to carry out the writer's own instructions improved the rewrite (Rijlaarsdam et al. 2006, abstract). These extend the earlier note's Traxler & Gernsbacher and Schriver findings.
- **Training helps in the trained form and transfers poorly.** A domain-tailored inspection technique beat a generic one, 25% against 18% (Kamsties). Interviewers at a site with more ambiguity-focused training caught more ambiguities live, 35% against 8%, confounded with site (Spoletini). A year of law school did not reduce the preference bias (Farnsworth). Forecasters' trained meanings did not carry outside forecasting (Wallsten, via Budescu). Two or more structured trauma courses raised closed-loop communication, while general team training did not (Härgestam et al. 2013, *BMJ Open* 3:e003525, abstract). Skill on others' texts did not carry to one's own (Bartlett), and general experience of ambiguity did not help speakers detect specific ones (Kraljic & Brennan).
- **There is a ceiling.** No method came near full coverage: 18–25% per review team, 32% live, weak agreement between pretesting methods. And most undetected ambiguities did no harm: 72% were read correctly anyway (Kamsties).

## 7. When an ambiguity matters, and the tension with not blocking

**Ambiguity is relative to the use the text is put to.** Gervasi, Ferrari, Zowghi & Spoletini (2019), "Ambiguity in requirements engineering: Towards a unifying framework", LNCS 11865 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-30985-5_12), original). A requirement ambiguous for implementation is unambiguous "for cost-estimate purposes" if all its readings cost the same. Their table of who recognized an ambiguity:

| Writer saw it | Reader saw it | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Yes | Yes | Legitimate abstraction, left open on purpose |
| Yes | No | Lost design space: the reader closed what the writer left open |
| No | Yes | The reader may ask, or may build the wrong thing |
| No | No | Correct only by chance |

*Standing:* conceptual, with cases. *Population:* people. One case: a pronoun was misresolved and caught only "when the analyst summarised what he understood".

**Nocuous ambiguity.** An ambiguity is "a property of text in relation to a set of stakeholders", and it is *nocuous* when readers' interpretations actually diverge. Chantree, Nuseibeh, de Roeck & Willis (2006), RE'06, and Yang, de Roeck, Willis & Nuseibeh (2010), COLING, pp. 1218–1226 ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/C10-1137.pdf), original for Yang). Seventeen professionals judged 138 coordination phrases from requirements, such as "security and privacy requirements". A phrase counted as nocuous if no reading exceeded a threshold set by the domain: high for safety-critical text, low for cookbooks. A classifier trained on these judgments reached about 75% accuracy. *Standing:* one research line. *Population:* people's judgments used to train a model. Gervasi et al. criticize it for ignoring the writer's intent.

**Words that change what people do.** Patients told a side effect was "common" (meant as 1–10%, actually 2.5%) estimated its likelihood at 34.2%, against 8.1% when given the number, and reported being less willing to take the medicine (Berry, Raynor, Knapp & Bersellini 2003, *Drug Safety* 26(1):1–11, abstract; Knapp, Raynor & Berry 2004, abstract). *Standing:* replicated across several studies. *Population:* people.

**Many ambiguous inputs have a dominant reading.** "She's from Boston" rarely means Boston, Georgia (Zhang & Choi 2023, section 8). Most ambiguous structures in a large corpus are resolved by shared cues (Roland et al. 2006). In Kamsties' experiments 72% of ambiguities were read correctly.

**The tension Manuel named.** He named a tension between not blocking on the human and a human who expects to be understood. The fields studied here frame it the same way and offer a threshold, not a rule.

- Senders prefer to send vague words, and receivers prefer precise ones (Wallsten et al. 1993, secondary via Budescu). Kent's precise odds table was rejected by the writers he called "poets". The writer's own comfort pulls toward vagueness.
- Gervasi's table names the cost on each side. A reader who silently resolves loses the writer's design space or builds the wrong thing. A reader who asks about everything pays for ambiguities that would have been read correctly.
- The nocuous threshold is the fields' answer: ask when readers would diverge enough that the actions differ beyond what the domain tolerates.
- People adjust how much they ask to how reliable the speaker is, even when asking earns them nothing. Current models do not (Bila et al. 2026, section 8).
- In models, committing early is associated with failure: on code and math, conversations where the model's first attempted answer came in the first fifth of the turns scored 30.9, against 64.4 when it came in the last fifth (Laban et al. 2025, original; a correlation). This adds a timing dimension to the earlier note's account of the same study.
- Anthropic's constitution says "Claude should ask for clarification in cases of genuine ambiguity", and lists among behaviors that make Claude "more annoying" that it "checks in or asks clarifying questions more than necessary for simple agentic tasks". It suggests serving both plausible readings when that is cheap, and raising questions before a task starts rather than midway ([constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution), original).
- Anthropic's guide for Claude Opus 5 gives a scope instruction: "Make routine judgment calls yourself, and check in only when different readings of the request would lead to materially different work" ([page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5), original, read 2026-10-05). Its guide for Opus 5.5 offers a system-prompt addition for unattended runs that makes the model carry on "where it would otherwise have stopped to check in", and advises leaving it out where "someone is there to answer" (original, read 2026-10-05).

## 8. Models noticing ambiguity

**Models notice when asked and rarely act on it.** This is the most replicated model finding in the area.

- Kuhn, Gal & Farquhar (2022), "CLAM", [arXiv:2212.07769](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07769), original. Left alone, GPT-3 models almost never asked, so their behavior separated ambiguous from clear questions at chance. A few-shot prompt asking whether the question was ambiguous separated them well. The authors guessed the cause was too few clarifying dialogues in training data, untested.
- Herlihy, Neville, Schnabel & Swaminathan (2024), "On overcoming miscalibrated conversational priors in LLM-based chatbots", UAI 2024, [arXiv:2406.01633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01633), original. More than 23% of 600 sampled real queries were severely underspecified, and GPT-4 still mostly answered directly. A chain-of-thought prompt behaved much like the baseline.
- Su & Cardie (2026), "Knowing but not showing", [arXiv:2605.25284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25284), original. Across ten models including Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4.1, 80–95% of responses to ambiguous questions were bare answers. The Claude family asked most, about 5%. Asked explicitly, most models labeled ambiguous questions correctly 60–80% of the time.
- Li, Kim & Wang (2025), "QuestBench", [arXiv:2503.22674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22674), original. On planning problems where 41.8% of cases lacked one needed fact, models said "not sure" on 0.7% (GPT-4o) to 4.6% (Claude 3.5 Sonnet). Being able to solve the fully specified version did not predict asking well.
- Kirichenko et al. (2025), "AbstentionBench", [arXiv:2506.09038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09038), original. Across 20 frontier models, reasoning traces contained more expressions of uncertainty while the final answer stayed definite. Reasoning fine-tuning cut abstention by 24% on average, and scale had almost no effect.
- Trinh et al. (2026), "HiL-Bench", [arXiv:2604.09408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09408), original. Coding and text-to-SQL tasks whose blockers appear only during exploration, on GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6. With full information, the models passed 64–91% of tasks. When each had to decide for itself whether to ask a human, the best reached 38% on SQL and 12% on coding. Claude was the only reasonably calibrated asker, and only on SQL. In 45% of Claude's failures of this kind, its reasoning trace said it was stuck and it submitted anyway. The authors call this detection without resolution. *Standing:* single study; the closest test to the factory's own model.
- Bila, Naszádi, Mayn & Monz (2026), "Build what I mean", [arXiv:2603.19997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19997), original. In a building task with one pragmatically reliable and one only literally reliable speaker, GPT-5.1 almost never asked. Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro asked mainly on underspecified trials, but about as often of the reliable speaker as of the unreliable one. People asked more of the literal speaker. *Population:* both.

*Standing:* replicated across groups, tasks and model generations from 2022 to 2026. *Population:* models.

**Why, layer one: the training signal discourages asking.** Shaikh, Gligorić, Khetan, Gerstgrasser, Yang & Jurafsky (2024), "Grounding gaps in language model generations", NAACL 2024, [arXiv:2311.09144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09144), original. Placed into real human dialogues, model turns were on average 77.5% less likely than the human turns to contain a grounding act such as a clarification or an acknowledgment. The causal part:

- On Mistral 7B, more supervised fine-tuning had no relation to grounding, while more preference optimization (DPO) lowered agreement with human grounding on all three acts measured (correlations −0.70 to −0.89).
- In preference-trained Llama 7B models, PPO lowered agreement by 39% and DPO by 42.5%.
- In the UltraFeedback preference dataset, preferred responses contained questions less often than rejected ones, 13.77% against 18.35%.

In Anthropic's HH-RLHF dataset, "provides direct advice instead of asking clarifying questions" raised the predicted win rate by 7%. Other datasets went the other way, so the effect depends on the dataset (Movva, Milli, Min & Pierson 2025, [arXiv:2510.26202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26202), original). Labeling preferences after a simulated second turn, instead of the first, improved models' asking (Zhang, Knox & Choi 2025, ICLR, [arXiv:2410.13788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13788), original). *Standing:* measured in two datasets and one training ablation. That it applies to frontier production models is inference, since no study saw proprietary post-training data. *Population:* models, trained on people's preferences.

**Why, layer two: the competing readings are never generated.** Liu et al. (2023), "We're afraid language models aren't modeling ambiguity" (AmbiEnt), EMNLP, [arXiv:2304.14399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14399), original. On 1,645 linguist-annotated ambiguous sentences, GPT-4's disambiguations were judged correct 32% of the time, against 90% for the dataset's own. On 76% of reading pairs, GPT-4 said a sentence may mean each reading and also that it can only mean each one. Further evidence:

- A model's answer probabilities track uncertainty about facts but not about what the question means. Counting how often sampled answers repeat was the best basis for deciding when to abstain, especially on ambiguous questions (Cole et al. 2023, EMNLP, [arXiv:2305.14613](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14613), original).
- Generating clarified versions of the input and answering each detected ambiguity far better than semantic entropy (AUROC 71.7 against 54.9, near chance). With the true clarifications supplied it reached 89.8. The bottleneck was generating the readings, not judging them (Hou et al. 2024, ICML, [arXiv:2311.08718](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08718), original).
- On ambiguous text-to-SQL questions, the best model found all interpretations for 1.9% of them, and chose one fixed structural reading in more than 97% of attachment ambiguities (Saparina & Lapata 2024, "AMBROSIA", NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks, [arXiv:2406.19073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19073), original).
- Sampling with beam search gives variety of tokens, not of interpretation (Bhaskar et al. 2023, [arXiv:2310.13659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13659), original).

*Standing:* the direction is replicated. *Population:* models (AmbiEnt also tested people). **What it explains:** this is the model counterpart of section 2. A reader that produces one reading has nothing to compare. It also explains why prompts to list readings help only as far as the model can produce the right ones.

**Prompting moves the threshold for asking, not the ability to choose when.** The signal-detection framing is my synthesis; each piece is separately sourced.

- A mitigation prompt quadrupled follow-up questions, while agreement with people on where to clarify fell to slightly below chance (Shaikh et al. 2024; the paper reports kappa going from 0 to −1.65, which looks like a ×100 scale, so check before quoting).
- Chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting gave marginal gains in spotting ambiguity and made small models overconfident. Most of ChatGPT's errors on self-contradictory queries were misses (Zhang et al. 2024, "CLAMBER", ACL, [arXiv:2405.12063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12063), original).
- Prompts mentioning ambiguity or doubt raised detection and lowered accuracy on unambiguous items. No model, including o3, scored high on both (Shore et al. 2025, "Correct-Detect", EMNLP, [arXiv:2509.14456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14456), original).
- Data-science agents on frontier models submitted an unflagged wrong answer on 39–63% of ambiguous runs. A permissive asking policy produced over-asking: 88% of Claude Haiku 4.5's questions on clear tasks were about style preferences that did not change the result. A conservative policy produced silent defaulting. No model and policy was calibrated on both (Stoisser et al. 2026, "Ambig-DS", [arXiv:2605.09698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09698), original).

*Standing:* replicated. *Population:* models. This parallels the human finding in section 4 that asking "is it ambiguous?" fails, but the cause differs: in people the question consults one reading, and in models the prompt shifts a trained willingness to ask.

**What works: sample readings, compare the actions, ask only when they differ.**

- ClarifyGPT samples several code solutions, generates test inputs and runs them, and asks only if the outputs differ. Its premise: a clear requirement yields varied code that behaves the same, and an unclear one yields code that behaves differently. In a human evaluation, GPT-4's pass rate on one benchmark rose from 70.96% to 80.80% (Mu et al. 2023, [arXiv:2310.10996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10996), original).
- KnowNo has a robot planner propose candidate actions, keeps every one above a calibrated threshold, and asks only if more than one remains. This gives a statistical guarantee of task success with as few questions as possible. Asking the model directly for its set of plausible options did worst (Ren et al. 2023, CoRL, [arXiv:2307.01928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01928), original).
- Writing a good question directly lost to sampling about 20 candidate solutions and picking the question that best splits them (Kobalczyk et al. 2025, ICLR, [arXiv:2502.04485](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04485), original). The authors argue that a question that tells readings apart is easier to write once the readings are written out. That echoes Prideaux & Baker's finding that people struggle to state the difference between two meanings.
- Intent-Sim simulates the user's possible answers to a clarifying question, groups them by meaning, and asks when the intents diverge. With a budget of asking on 10% of inputs, it doubled the gain over choosing at random (Zhang & Choi 2023, [arXiv:2311.09469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09469), original).

*Standing:* several independent methods, each a single line of work. *Population:* models. **What it explains:** these methods get around both causes above. Sampling produces several readings without trusting a single output's likelihood, and comparing actions keeps only the differences that matter. Ambig-DS's style-preference over-asking shows what happens without that filter. These are Manuel's skilled-listener test as an algorithm, and the human counterpart is the second reader of section 5.

**Training models to ask works, so far only on small open models.** Tamkin et al. (2022, [arXiv:2212.10711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10711)) fine-tuned GPT-3 on 272 ambiguous tasks and closed its gap with a human-feedback model, while an unambiguous control set did nothing. STaR-GATE (Andukuri et al. 2024, [arXiv:2403.19154](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19154)), ACT (Chen et al. 2025, ICLR, [arXiv:2406.00222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00222)), CollabLLM (Wu et al. 2025, ICML, [arXiv:2502.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00640)), and HiL-Bench's reinforcement learning on a 32B model all improved asking. All read as originals. These four reward the outcome after a later turn rather than the next turn alone, which answers the training cause above. *Standing:* consistent. *Population:* models from 7B to 32B. Transfer to frontier models is untested in public work.

A related result on false premises: pretrained models failed questions with false premises while holding the knowledge to rebut them. Fine-tuning on about 256 such examples, with ordinary questions mixed back in, made the model catch 86.7% of false-premise questions while wrongly challenging 1.4% of ordinary ones (Hu et al. 2023, "Won't get fooled again", ACL, [arXiv:2307.02394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02394), original). This parallels the human finding that showing the kind of problem helps (Baker 1984), and it reports the false alarms that Kamas et al. warn about.

**Ambiguity in text a model wrote.** No study found tests whether a model detects ambiguity in its own output, or in instructions it writes for another model. The nearest: one agent fails to pass on a requirement and the receiver proceeds on a wrong assumption instead of asking (Cemri et al. 2025, already in the earlier note). Models checking requirements written by others found a median 47% of the issues experts found (Shefa et al. 2026, [arXiv:2609.03230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03230), abstract). The same ambiguous requirement produced functionally different implementations across models (Yang et al. 2026, "Orchid", [arXiv:2604.21505](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21505), abstract and introduction). My inference: ClarifyGPT's test applies to any text. A writer could have fresh readers act on its draft and compare what they would do. Nobody has tested a model doing this to its own writing.

## 9. Where people and models line up and where they differ

- **Same shape: the second reading is never produced.** People select one reading below awareness (section 2), and models collapse onto the most common reading (section 8). In both, judging ambiguity gets easy once both readings are on the table, and producing them is the hard step.
- **Same shape: knowing differs from acting.** People's hidden meaning leaks into their behavior unnoticed (MacKay), and models label ambiguity when asked but don't act on it (CLAM; HiL-Bench's "detection without resolution").
- **Different causes for not asking.** In people, the threshold is bent by preference, by being in the majority, by confidence in a settled reading (section 4), and by the need for closure under time pressure and fatigue (section 6). In models, the measured cause is training that rewards answering. A remedy aimed at one cause may not reach the other.
- **Urging behaves the same way.** In both, an instruction to look for problems moves the threshold and not the accuracy: people urged to catch distortions flagged more of everything (Kamas et al. 1996), and models prompted to consider ambiguity asked more everywhere (Shaikh et al.; Correct-Detect). In both, what improves accuracy supplies a candidate: a named standard, an alternative diagnosis or a sampled reading.
- **Listing readings is capped by what can be produced.** People who know both meanings of a word still fail to report both meanings of a sentence (Kamowski-Shakibai & Cairns 2016), and people find it hard to state two meanings distinctly (Prideaux & Baker 1976). Models asked to list readings produce the right ones about a third of the time (AmbiEnt). In both, a list the reader writes alone is limited by the reader.
- **The remedy is shared.** Independent readings compared by the actions they lead to work in both: a second reader (Gause & Weinberg; Spoletini) and sampled readings compared by their outputs (ClarifyGPT; KnowNo).
- **Writer-side blindness in models is untested.** The human evidence that writers cannot see their own ambiguity is strong (section 3). Whether a model writing a brief has the same blindness is a hypothesis. Juba et al.'s formal argument predicts it for any writer that decodes with the prior it encoded with.

## 10. What this means for the map's decisions

This section is my reading of how the evidence bears on the map's tickets. It is not a finding, and the tickets own the decisions. I state tensions where the evidence cuts both ways.

**The writing standard (#155), organized around capacities of mind.** The evidence puts noticing ambiguity in two of the three capacities the ticket names. Reading another mind needs the reader to produce more than one reading, and examining one's own needs the writer to see past its own intent. The evidence says these do not happen by intention. A standard that says "notice ambiguity" or "check whether this is clear" asks for exactly the introspection that fails in people (Farnsworth; Kamsties). It is also the kind of urging that moves the threshold without improving accuracy, in people (Kamas et al.; Norman et al.) and in models (Shaikh; Correct-Detect). What worked in both is a procedure that produces the other reading:

- reading the text as a different reader would, rather than asking whether it is clear;
- writing out the readings and the action each leads to, which is Manuel's skilled-listener step;
- forcing the words into a form with no room for vagueness: a number, a test case, a concrete example of the result.

**The writer's side.** The strongest single finding for writing is own-text blindness (Bartlett; Ferreira; Keysar 1994). A writer checking its own brief is the reader least able to see its ambiguity. That supports a check by a reader that lacks the writer's context over a self-check. For a factory that runs many lanes, the second reader is cheap. A fresh lane asked to restate a brief and say what it would do is a teach-back, not a read-back.

**The tension between not blocking and being understood.** The fields' answer is a threshold based on consequence: ask, or flag, when readings would lead to actions that differ beyond what the task tolerates, not whenever a second reading exists. That is the nocuous test, Anthropic's Opus 5 wording, and the action filter in ClarifyGPT and KnowNo. Without that filter, a model asks about style (Ambig-DS). With no willingness to ask, it defaults silently. The constitution's option of serving both readings when that is cheap is a third path the factory could use where asking would block.

**Manuel's account of how a misread intent became a rule.** He described a model that "didnt stop to say 'He didnt say exactly this, I shouldnt assume I know what he wants/means'". The evidence predicts this. The model held one reading of "I saw its effect somewhere else", and nothing inside it signaled another. It also predicts that a later instruction to "notice assumptions" would do little on its own, or would make the agent flag everything. What the evidence supports is a moment at which the agent writes out what else the words could mean and what it would do under each, before acting on a correction or writing a rule. The children's training studies add one detail: an action rule tied to a condition ("ask when the message fits more than one thing") worked and lasted, while a rule to compare more carefully did not (Patterson et al. 1978).

**Where the guidance lives and when it arrives (#156).** Four findings point the same way:

- Task demands set processing depth (Swets; Christianson).
- The alternative meaning had to be in the reader's path, not in an optional lookup (Budescu; Wintle).
- Alternatives helped before a first diagnosis formed and not after (Kostopoulou et al., replicated). Accountability helped before a decision and turned defensive after (Lerner & Tetlock).
- Doctors consulted an optional aid about 2% of the time (Kostopoulou et al.).

Together they favor guidance that arrives at the moment of reading or writing, before the reader commits, triggered by the situation rather than by the agent deciding it needs help. That matches what the earlier note found for perspective-taking. For Manuel's question about external enforcement, the last finding is the strongest: a habit the agent must remember to invoke is the kind people fail to invoke.

**How we'll know it works (#153).** The fields offer measures that transfer:

- Count the readings a reader produces for a known-ambiguous text (AmbiEnt).
- Measure detection and false alarms together, since urging trades one for the other in people (Kamas et al.) and in models (Correct-Detect).
- Keep "noticed" and "acted better" as separate outcomes, since one can replicate while the other does not (Crawford & Ruscio; HiL-Bench's detection without resolution).
- Run the factory's own version of ClarifyGPT: give the same brief to several fresh lanes and compare what they would do.

## 11. What the evidence leaves open

- **Claude Opus 5 and 5.5.** No published study tests them on noticing or asking. The nearest are Opus 4.6 (HiL-Bench), Opus 4.5 (Bila et al.), Sonnet 4.6 and Haiku 4.5.
- **A model detecting ambiguity in its own writing**, or in a brief it writes for another model. Untested. The human evidence predicts it is hard. The factory can test it: have a model write a brief, give the brief to several fresh lanes, and compare their plans.
- **Whether a general habit transfers.** No study measures whether people or models can learn a general habit of asking "is there a reading that leads to a different action?" and carry it to new material. The transfer evidence in people is mostly negative (Bartlett; Kraljic & Brennan; Farnsworth; Asher & Wigfield). The exceptions are debiasing games trained with feedback (Morewedge et al.; Sellier et al.) and riddle training that carried over to inconsistencies in stories (Zipke et al.).
- **Whether adults, like children, collapse to one guess under uncertainty about what someone meant** (Robinson et al. 2006). Not checked.
- **Whether a model has anything like the need for closure.** Untested. Early commitment goes with failure in models (Laban et al.), but no study manipulates the conditions that raise closure in people.
- **Frontier-model training for asking.** Public results stop at 32B open models.
- **Human annotators preferring presumptuous answers.** Asserted by several model papers and supported by dataset statistics, not measured directly.
- **Manuel's step of stating the difference in words.** Supported indirectly: questions that split candidate solutions are easier to write once the solutions exist (Kobalczyk et al.), and people find it hard to paraphrase two meanings distinctly (Prideaux & Baker). No study tests the step itself as an intervention.

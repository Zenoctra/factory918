# How a mind finds the causes of its own mistakes

Research for ticket #162 on the map "Write to agents with theory of mind" (#146), 2026-10-05. It extends [the note on wording and reader context](https://github.com/Zenoctra/factory918/blob/research/wording-and-reader-context/docs/research/wording-and-reader-context.md) (#154) and does not repeat it.

A mind's account of why it did something is a theory it builds about itself, using the same capacity it uses to read other minds, and it catches its own mistakes only when a rival reading is active at the moment it checks. Looking back after a correction then pulls it toward "what rule would have prevented this" instead of "what made the misreading likely", and the evidence says this holds for people and, by different machinery, for language models.

## How to read this note

**What it covers.** Introspection and self-report research, metacognition, error detection, judgment and decision research, diagnostic error in medicine, text comprehension, cognitive neuroscience of error monitoring, safety science and accident investigation, organizational learning, intelligence analysis, training research, and language-model research on introspection, self-explanation, self-correction, failure attribution and agent memory. The question was what these fields know about three things: how a mind finds the causes of its own mistakes, how far its account of its own reasons can be trusted, and what models do in the same position.

**How it was done.** Four reading lanes read the sources, each told to trace claims to the original and to follow every effect to the work that explains it. I checked the load-bearing model numbers against the paper text the lanes saved. Every entry says what was actually read:

- *original*: the paper, chapter or system card itself, or full-text passages of it.
- *abstract*: only the original's abstract.
- *secondary*: someone else's account, named where it matters. Treat these numbers as unconfirmed.

Where a number came through a search or fetch tool that summarizes pages rather than giving the page text, the entry says so. Check those before quoting them anywhere that matters. The lanes' shared search budget ran out partway through, so several entries rest on abstracts.

**Labels.** The same two labels as the earlier note, so the notes read side by side.

- *Standing*: **replicated**, **narrowed**, **contested**, **failed**, or **single study**, as before. This note adds **argued** for theory, doctrine or case work that was never tested under controls. Much of safety science and organizational learning is argued, and that should be visible.
- *Population*: **people**, **organizations**, **models**, or **both**. A finding about people is a hypothesis about models until someone tests it on models, and the reverse.

**How this note is organized, and why.** Manuel asked that a source be checked for whether another work explains it before it is cited: "a different study that explains WHY the failure mode exists or a different layer to what causes it is worth way more." So the sections below are built around the accounts that explain the most findings, and each demonstration sits under the account it is an instance of. The grouping is my reading of the evidence, not a complete list of how self-examination goes wrong. A reader who treats the headings as the full space will miss what they don't name.

**Terms used throughout.**

- *Introspection* here means a mind reporting on its own states, reasons or processes. *Metacognition* is a mind monitoring and controlling its own thinking: confidence, the sense of having understood, the decision to check again.
- *Confabulation* is producing a sincere, plausible account of one's reasons that is not what produced the behavior.
- A *slip* is an action that departs from a correct intention. A *mistake* is a correct execution of a wrong intention or interpretation. This is Reason's distinction, used loosely by the sources below.
- *Effect size d* is the difference between two groups in standard deviations. By convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. *g* is the same with a small-sample correction.
- A *lane* is a subagent started on a job by an orchestrator.

## The short version

1. **A mind's account of its own reasons is an inference, not a readout, and it is built by the same capacity that reads other minds.** People's reports of what influenced them matched the guesses of observers who read only a summary of the situation (r = .77 to .99). People defend choices they never made with reasons that look like real ones. Developmental, philosophical and computational accounts converge on one mechanism: the self is read by self-interpretation. Models show the same split: Claude described adding 36 + 59 by carrying the 1, while its circuits did something else. Introspection is therefore part of theory of mind, as Manuel ruled on #155, and it inherits theory of mind's failure modes. (Replicated in people. Several independent lines in models.)

2. **A report is trustworthy only for what was in attention at the time. An assumption, by definition, was not.** Thinking aloud about what you are attending to does not change performance (r = −.03 across 94 studies). Asking why forces inference, because the selection of one thought over another never passed through attention. When the conditions are right, people report their own decision weights well (r = .57 to .86). An assumption is an interpretation chosen without being attended to, so it cannot be introspected directly. It can only be inferred from traces, such as the phrase that was misread. This is the human evidence for the point Manuel accepted on #155: the explanation lands in a mind, while the data is often external. (Replicated in people.)

3. **A mind catches its own mistake only when a rival representation is active at the moment it checks.** Students caught 83% of their calculation slips, 48% of their method errors and none of their skipped steps. Readers told to hunt for contradictions missed them when the two statements were far apart or paraphrased. Mechanics did not notice when half the causes on a fault tree were missing. Error-monitoring neuroscience, reasoning research, text comprehension and judgment research each reach this account from their own data. Rechecking with the same reading produces agreement, not doubt. "Not even realizing you had an assumption", Manuel's phrase, is the state this account predicts, not a lapse of effort. Models show it sharply: the same model fixes an error presented as the user's and misses it as its own (64.5% average gap across 14 models), and over 90% of reasoning models' reflections confirm the first answer. (Replicated across fields in people. Several groups in models.)

4. **The feeling that should trigger a check is produced by the same misreading.** Confidence and the feeling of rightness come from how fluently and consistently an answer arrives, not from whether it is right. Confidence correlated negatively with accuracy on questions most people get wrong. So "ask when unsure" fails in exactly the cases that matter: a fluent misreading feels sure. Models have the parallel: their own text is the most probable text to them, and judges rate low-perplexity text higher whoever wrote it. (Replicated in people. Replicated in models.)

5. **Looking back after a correction pulls toward a prevention rule, not a cause.** Asking "how could it have been prevented" selects controllable antecedents. Asking "what caused it" selects antecedents that covaried with the outcome, and the two answers were unrelated. Causal analysis stops when it reaches a familiar cause or "a cure is available". Hindsight makes the mistake look foreseeable, and reviewers who knew an error had happened found twice as many biases in the same workup, with essentially no agreement on which. Writing Manuel's corrected intent into a rule is this pattern exactly: a controllable fix, available at once, aimed at the one case. (Replicated in people for hindsight and outcome bias. The prevention-versus-cause dissociation is one lab's program. The stop rule is argued, with one interview study behind it.)

6. **These fields say there is no natural rock bottom. They offer a stopping criterion instead: stop when the cause names a type, not an instance, and the fix changes what sets the behavior.** Rasmussen put it as "fighting types, not individual tokens", and fixing tasks one by one as cooling a thermostat-heated room "by opening the window". The counterweights are real: in simple one-cause events, going deeper did not help, and a review of "triple-loop" learning cautions against an "uncritical preference for 'higher levels'". (Argued, with archival support.)

7. **Lessons transfer when they come from comparing varied cases, and over-fit when they come from one.** Students who compared two cases were about three times as likely to use the principle later. Airlines whose past accidents had varied causes had fewer later accidents. Error training paid off on structurally different tasks (d = 0.80) far more than on similar ones (d = 0.20). Lessons from one case get applied on surface similarity and do harm. In models, written lessons lose their applicability conditions as they are rewritten: one model failed 54% of problems it had solved before, after consolidating lessons from correct solutions. Manuel's test, "how many different failure modes can I attribute to a single set of craft theory", has its strongest support here. (Replicated in people. Single but recent studies in models.)

8. **What helps is outside signal and changed cues, not exhortation.** Debriefs improve performance (d = 0.67 to 0.79), and every debrief in the main meta-analysis had a partner, facilitator or recording. "Be thorough" did nothing (45.0% against 44.5%). Teaching people to recognize biases did nothing. Deliberate reflection helped a little (g = 0.20), when it forced new alternatives into view. Explaining how something works exposed gaps, while justifying a belief strengthened it. In models, a forced counterfactual step raised accuracy at finding the decisive step in a failed agent trace from 17% to 47%, and a sound external verifier helped where self-critique hurt. (Mixed standing, given per finding.)

## 1. The account of one's own reasons is an inference

**The founding claim.** Nisbett & Wilson (1977), "Telling more than we can know: Verbal reports on mental processes", *Psychological Review* 84(3):231–259 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231), original). Shoppers chose among four identical pairs of stockings and preferred the right-most pair almost four to one. No one mentioned position, and almost all denied it when asked. In a second study, participants' reports of what influenced their judgments correlated .77 to .99 with the predictions of observers who only read a summary of the factors. Where reports were accurate, observers did as well (r = .94 for participants, .98 for observers). For liking, sympathy and flexibility, accuracy was "literally nil" (r = −.31, .14, .11). Their account: reports of causes come from "a priori, implicit causal theories", judgments of what would plausibly cause such a response. A report is accurate when the real cause also happens to be plausible. *Standing:* contested and narrowed (below). *Population:* people.

White (1988), *British Journal of Psychology* 79:13–45 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1988.tb02271.x), abstract), found that "process" was never defined well, that the observer-group logic had serious problems, and that ten years of work gave no unambiguous support or falsification of even a weak form of the claim.

**When reports are valid.** Ericsson & Simon's protocol analysis drew the boundary. Read through Ericsson (2006), *Cambridge Handbook of Expertise and Expert Performance*, ch. 13 ([PDF](https://www.ida.liu.se/~nilda08/Anders_Ericsson/Ericsson_protocol.pdf), original); the 1980 *Psychological Review* paper was not reached. A person who speaks aloud thoughts already in attention, without describing or explaining them, gives a valid report. The meta-analysis by Fox, Ericsson & Best (2011), *Psychological Bulletin* 137(2):316–344 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021663), abstract), found across 94 studies and about 3,500 participants that thinking aloud this way left performance unchanged (r = −.03). Instructions to describe or explain changed performance. Retrospective reports are valid right after tasks of a few seconds. For processes longer than 10 to 30 seconds, people "are increasingly tempted to infer what they must have thought." On why-questions, the line that matters most here: because people can access only the end products of thought, "they cannot report why only one of several logically possible thoughts entered their attention, they must make inferences or confabulate answers." *Standing:* the non-reactivity claim is replicated meta-analytically. *Population:* people.

**When the conditions are right, self-report is good.** Morris, Carlson, Kober & Crockett (2025), "Introspective access to value-based multi-attribute choice processes", *Nature Communications* 16 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59080-y), original). Across five studies, people's reported attribute weights correlated .57 to .86 with weights fitted from their choices, and a third to a half reported their whole choice method correctly. They beat observers paid for accuracy by 27 to 78% in correlation. 170 decision scientists predicted a correlation of .44; the observed figure was .80. Individual accuracy ranged "from perfect to worse than chance", and nothing measured predicted it, including a self-reported insight scale and cognitive ability. *Standing:* single paper with internal replications. *Population:* people. Its conditions are the ones Ericsson says should work: the attributes sat explicitly in a table, the choice was deliberate, the report immediate. People also predicted the pattern of their own implicit-attitude test results at a corrected r = .56 across 17 replications (Goedderz, Rahmani Azad & Hahn 2023, [PsyArXiv](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/frwcy), abstract; replicated within one lab).

**Choice blindness: reasons for choices never made.** Johansson, Hall, Sikström & Olsson (2005), *Science* 310:116–119 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709), abstract, numbers via Petitmengin et al. 2013). Participants chose the more attractive of two faces, and on some trials were handed the one they rejected. About a quarter of switches were detected by any measure. Johansson et al. (2006), *Consciousness and Cognition* 15:673–692 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2006.09.004), original), compared the reasons people gave for choices they had and had not made. On hedges, pauses, specificity and semantic measures they were nearly indistinguishable. The effect has appeared with jam and tea, moral attitudes (69% missed at least one of two reversals) and political attitudes (92% endorsed an altered score) (Hall et al. 2010, 2012, 2013, abstracts). Strandberg et al. (2018), *JEP: General* 147:1382–1399 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000489), abstract): people who argued for an altered political position kept the shifted attitude for at least a week, and the shift was larger for those who wrote arguments. Replicated in a second experiment (N = 232). Confabulated reasons are adopted, not just produced.

*Standing:* the failure to report is replicated many times. What it means is contested. Grassi et al. (2025), *Frontiers in Psychology* 16:1598254 (original, n = 41 and 23) found pupils dilated on all manipulated trials whether reported or not, and people picked out 84% of manipulations afterwards while reporting 56% at the time. They read it as covert detection plus reluctance to report. Rieznik et al. (2017, *PLOS ONE* e0171108, abstract) found lower confidence on undetected manipulated trials. Petitmengin et al. (2013), *Consciousness and Cognition* 22(2):654–669 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2013.02.004), original preprint), raised detection from 33% to 80% with an interview that repeatedly asked people to recall the moment of choice. Small sample, and the interviewer was not blind. *Population:* people.

**Analyzing reasons changes what it analyzes.** Wilson & Dunn (2004), "Self-knowledge: its limits, value, and potential for improvement", *Annual Review of Psychology* 55:493–518 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.141954), original). Analyzing one's reasons lowered satisfaction with choices, how well people predicted their own behavior and how well stated attitudes predicted later behavior. The mechanism they give: people "generate reasons that are consistent with cultural and personal theories and are accessible in memory", do not see that these reasons are incomplete, and "construct a new attitude ... consistent with the reasons that happen to come to mind." Their remedy is to look outward: observe one's own behavior and see oneself through others' eyes. *Standing:* replicated mostly within one lab. I found no independent registered replication. The related verbal-overshadowing effect replicated in a registered multi-lab study but smaller and dependent on timing (Alogna et al. 2014, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 9:556–578, abstract). *Population:* people.

**Self-knowledge is self-interpretation.** Several independent lines reach the same claim.

- Gopnik (1993), "How we know our minds", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 16:1–14 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00028636), abstract). Three-year-olds misreport their own just-past beliefs in the same way they misreport other people's. The felt directness of self-knowledge "may be analogous to many cases where expertise provides an illusion of direct perception." Gopnik & Astington (1988, *Child Development* 59:26–37, abstract) found reporting one's own past belief was harder than reporting another's false belief, and the two correlated.
- Carruthers' interpretive sensory-access theory (*BBS* 32:121–138, 2009; *The Opacity of Mind*, 2011; read through Byrne's 2012 review, secondary). One faculty attributes mental states to self and others. Access to sensations, imagery and inner speech is direct. Access to one's own judgments, decisions, beliefs and intentions is interpretive.
- Fleming & Daw (2017), "Self-evaluation of decision-making: a general Bayesian framework for metacognitive computation", *Psychological Review* 124:91–114 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000045), abstract). Self-evaluation is modeled as second-order inference about a coupled decision system, "computationally equivalent to inferring the performance of another actor."
- Koriat's cue-utilization account (Koriat & Ackerman 2010, *Consciousness and Cognition* 19:251–264, original; Koriat 1997 not reached). Metacognitive judgments are inferences from cues: features of the material, conditions of study, and how easily something comes to mind. People used their own felt effort as a cue for themselves, and applied it to another person only after experiencing it. So the self has cues an observer lacks, and still infers.

*Standing:* theory, with replicated developmental parallels. Contested by the cases where self beats observer: Morris et al. 2025, the implicit-attitude predictions, and Vazire (2010), *JPSP* 98:281–300 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017908), abstract), who found the self was the best judge of traits that are hard to observe and not evaluative, friends were better for evaluative ones, and all were equal for visible ones. The reading that fits all of it: the self has more and better cues, such as its own effort and recent episodes, and still reaches its reasons by inference. Morris and colleagues say they cannot exclude this. *Population:* people.

**Why it feels like direct access.** Schwitzgebel (2008), "The unreliability of naive introspection", *Philosophical Review* 117:245–273 (original preprint), argues people err grossly even about current experience, and that "no one ever scolds us for getting it wrong ... This lack of corrective feedback encourages a hypertrophy of confidence." Rozenblit & Keil (2002), "The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth", *Cognitive Science* 26:521–562 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1), original), give the same reason for why people think they understand mechanisms: explanations have no clear end point, and people rarely produce them, so they rarely get feedback. Asking for a mechanistic explanation reduced the illusion; asking for reasons did not (Fernbach et al. 2013, *Psychological Science* 24:939–946, abstract). Three preregistered replications confirmed the drop in felt understanding, failed to replicate its effect on political views, and replicated that "providing justifications for prior beliefs strengthens those beliefs" (Crawford & Ruscio 2021, *Psychological Science* 32:611–621, abstract). *Standing:* the core illusion replicated; downstream effects failed. *Population:* people.

**The bias blind spot.** People judge their own bias by introspecting on their intentions and other people's by their behavior, and think that weighting is correct (Pronin & Kugler 2007, *JESP* 43:565–578, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2006.05.011), secondary via Cheek & Pronin 2021). Three preregistered studies with N = 969 found a large self-other gap (d = −1.00) (Chandrashekar et al. 2023, *Judgment and Decision Making*; numbers through a summarizing tool). Cognitive ability does not reduce it, and being free of the blind spot does not reduce actual bias (West, Meserve & Stanovich 2012, *JPSP* 103:506–519, abstract). One result bears on how the factory might teach this: teaching people about bias did not reduce the blind spot, while reading about the limits of introspection did (Pronin & Kugler 2007, single study, secondary). *Standing:* the blind spot replicated; the intervention single study. *Population:* people.

**Why minds produce reasons at all.** Functional accounts explain why the inference runs toward justification. Mercier & Sperber (2011), *BBS* 34:57–74 (abstract): reasoning evolved to produce and evaluate arguments for others, so it looks for support for a conclusion already held. Cushman (2020), "Rationalization is rational", *BBS* 43:e28 (abstract): rationalization invents the beliefs and desires that would have made an action sensible, and people then adopt them, moving information from habit and instinct into a format reasoning can use. Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere "interpreter" (2000, *Brain* 123:1293–1326, abstract) is the clinical version, resting on a few split-brain patients. Turner & Coltheart (2010), *Cognitive Neuropsychiatry* 15:346–376 (abstract), locate confabulation at a checking stage: plausible content is produced and never checked. *Standing:* argued, with interpretive evidence. *Population:* people.

**What this means for the question.** When Claude explains why it misread Manuel, the explanation is a plausible theory of the kind a mind produces about another mind, and nothing in the evidence suggests it is privileged. The trustworthy route to an internal cause is the one that also works on others: inference from traces of what happened, done on purpose. Wilson and Dunn's remedy, look at your behavior as an observer would, is the human form. This also sharpens Manuel's distinction between external and internal: the target of the search is internal (what in my reading made this make sense), and the evidence is often external (the phrase, the order of the messages, what I wrote next).

## 2. A mind catches its own mistake only when a rival is active

**Which errors people catch.** Allwood (1984), "Error detection processes in statistical problem solving", *Cognitive Science* 8(4):413–437 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0804_5)). 16 students solved problems aloud and made 327 errors. They caught 83% of execution errors, 48% of errors in solution method, 25% of higher-level errors and none of their skipped steps. Detection began when a result felt "dissatisfying or strange", or from routine checks. Many undetected errors raised no suspicion at all. Detection rates secondary, via Panko (2008, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.3613); 2015, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.02601)); one percentage came through a search snippet. *Standing:* single study. *Population:* people. Sellen (1994), *Applied Psychology* 43(4):475–498 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1464-0597.1994.tb00841.x), abstract), sorted about 600 everyday errors by how they were caught: by noticing the action, by noticing the outcome, or by the world blocking the action. Reason's *Human Error* (1990), the standard source for the claim that mistakes are caught far less often than slips, was not read.

**The mechanism in error monitoring.** Yeung, Botvinick & Cohen (2004), *Psychological Review* 111:931–959 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295x.111.4.939), abstract), and Yeung & Summerfield (2012), *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 367:1310–1321 ([PMC3318764](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3318764/), original). People detect errors through conflict after the response: processing continues, the correct response becomes active, and it clashes with the one already made. Rabbitt's classic work, cited there, found self-detection reliable without feedback but impaired when the stimulus was too brief for processing to continue. Conscious awareness of an error is a later, separate stage, and unaware errors show no slowing afterwards. Error detection and confidence share one graded signal (Boldt & Yeung 2015, *Journal of Neuroscience* 35:3478, abstract). *Standing:* replicated across many labs. Yeung and Summerfield doubt the principles scale up from simple choices to real decisions. *Population:* people. A slip leaves the correct intention active to clash with the action. A mistaken interpretation leaves nothing to clash with, because the recheck runs on the same interpretation.

**The mechanism in reasoning.** De Neys (2023), "Advancing theorizing about fast-and-slow thinking", *BBS* 46:e111 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X2200142X), original). Deliberation starts when two competing intuitions are close enough in strength to create uncertainty. If a problem cues "one single intuition" strongly, "the cued response can be selected without further deliberation." The other trigger is outside feedback that violates an expectation, which "only works in case you're actually getting feedback." Pennycook, Fugelsang & Koehler (2015), *Cognitive Psychology* 80:34–72 (abstract): when deliberation does start, it often checks the first answer after the fact instead of rejecting it. Thompson, Prowse Turner & Pennycook (2011), *Cognitive Psychology* 63:107 (abstract): a low feeling of rightness predicted longer rethinking and more changed answers, and the feeling came from how fluently the first answer arrived. *Standing:* the model is a 2023 proposal. Conflict detection is contested, with null findings, and varies between people (Frey, Johnson & De Neys 2018, abstract). *Population:* people.

**The mechanism in reading.** Glenberg, Wilkinson & Epstein (1982), "The illusion of knowing", *Memory & Cognition* 10:597–602 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03202442), original). Readers told to look for contradictions missed them and rated their comprehension high. In a pilot, 95% missed the contradiction and were confident, and none said afterwards they had noticed and kept quiet. Misses rose when the contradicting sentence was marked as new information, and in longer texts (25% against 3%). Epstein, Glenberg & Bradley (1984), *Memory & Cognition* 12:355–360 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198295), original): misses rose when the contradiction needed an inference, sat in separate paragraphs, or was paraphrased. Readers who missed contradictions recalled only one of the two statements, or explained the clash away (Otero & Kintsch 1992, *Psychological Science* 3:229, search snippet of the abstract). Misconceptions are revised only when old and new information are active together (Kendeou & O'Brien 2014, KReC framework, search snippet). Speakers stopped reusing an ambiguous word for a different meaning only after they had produced it once, that is, after the second meaning had been activated (Ferreira, Slevc & Rogers 2005, *Cognition* 96:263, abstract). *Standing:* robust in this literature. *Population:* people. This is the closest lab analogue I found to misreading an instruction without knowing it.

**Unconsidered hypotheses are invisible.** Fischhoff, Slovic & Lichtenstein (1978), "Fault trees", *JEP: Human Perception and Performance* 4:330–344 ([PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/prediction/1978-fischhoff.pdf), original). When half the branches of a "car won't start" fault tree were removed, the probability people gave to "other" should have risen from .078 to .468. It only doubled. Experienced mechanics were about as insensitive as lay people. Hirt & Castellan (1988, abstract) replicated it and argued people silently stretch the remaining categories to absorb the missing causes. Either way, the gap goes unnoticed. HyGene (Thomas, Dougherty, Sprenger & Harbison 2008, *Psychological Review* 115:155, abstract) models judgment as comparing the favored hypothesis with alternatives retrieved from memory: fewer are retrieved under time pressure, and errors in generating them "cascade" into judgment errors. The chess Einstellung study in the earlier note (section 2) is the eye-tracking version. *Standing:* replicated. *Population:* people.

**In the field.** Cook & Woods (1994), "Operating at the sharp end", in Bogner (ed.), *Human Error in Medicine*, 255–310 ([PDF](https://securityandtechnology.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/operatingatthesharp-2.pdf), original). Fixation is failing to revise an assessment as evidence arrives, and the first assessment is usually appropriate to the early, partial data. In garden-path problems, "early cues strongly suggest [plausible but] incorrect answers, and later, usually weaker cues suggest answers that are correct." These are "not due to knowledge factors." On hindsight: "After the fact ... the solution seems obvious, even to the fixated person." In diagnosis, premature closure ("failure to continue considering reasonable alternatives after an initial diagnosis was reached") was the most common cognitive cause across 100 cases of diagnostic error (Graber, Franklin & Gordon 2005, *Archives of Internal Medicine* 165:1493–1499, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.165.13.1493), abstract; the labels were assigned by reviewers who knew the outcome, see section 4). In complex cases, a salient but irrelevant finding placed early cut correct diagnoses from 43% to 18%, and the same finding placed last had no effect (Mamede et al. 2014, *Academic Medicine* 89:114–120, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000000077), abstract, single study). *Population:* people.

**What this means for the question.** Manuel asked how the agent could have noticed it was assuming. On this evidence, noticing needs a second reading of the request to be active at the moment of reading, or an outside signal. Effort, care and rereading do not supply one. The risky moments have a recognizable structure: an early strong cue, a short message, material the reader had to infer, a phrase that admits more than one reading. That matches Manuel's account of how a careful listener works, asking whether there are "multiple understandings of the input" that would lead to "radically different reactions". On this evidence that habit works because it creates the rival reading the account says is missing. Ambiguity detection itself belongs to a sibling ticket.

## 3. The feeling of being right comes from the misreading

**Confidence tracks consensus and consistency, not truth.** Koriat (2008), "Subjective confidence in one's answers: the consensuality principle", *JEP: LMC* 34:945–959 (abstract). The correlation between confidence and accuracy was positive for questions most people answer correctly, and negative for questions most people answer wrongly. Koriat (2012), *Psychological Review* 119:80–113 (abstract): confidence measures how consistently the retrieved evidence supports the answer, which is reliability, not validity, and overconfidence lives in the gap. Ackerman & Thompson (2017), "Meta-reasoning", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 21:607 (abstract): monitoring feelings "are based on heuristic cues, which are not necessarily reliable", yet they decide how much effort a problem gets. Kahneman & Klein (2009), "Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree", *American Psychologist* 64:515–526 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755), abstract): "subjective experience is not a reliable indicator of judgment accuracy." Intuition earns trust only where the environment is regular and offers a chance to learn it. *Standing:* the cue-based view is the mainstream position in metacognition. Within it, effects of perceptual fluency (hard-to-read fonts) failed to replicate across 17 experiments (Meyer et al. 2015, *JEP: General* 144:e16, abstract). Fluency of the answer itself holds. *Population:* people.

**Doctors' confidence barely moves with difficulty.** Costa Filho et al. (2019, *Perspectives on Medical Education* 8:230, abstract): structured reflection raised diagnostic accuracy from 38% to 50% while confidence and calibration did not change. *Population:* people.

**What this means for the question.** A rule that says "ask when unsure" is triggered by a feeling that the misreading itself produces. A fluent misreading feels sure. This is why Manuel's observation that assumptions are hard to notice holds even for careful minds: the signal that would start the check is generated downstream of the error. The trigger has to come from somewhere other than the feeling, such as the structure of the moment (section 2) or an outside signal.

## 4. Looking back after a correction

**Prevention is not cause.** Mandel & Lehman (1996), "Counterfactual thinking and ascriptions of cause and preventability", *JPSP* 71(3):450–463 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.71.3.450), abstract). Across three studies, "if only" thoughts and judgments of how an outcome could have been prevented focused on controllable antecedents. Causal judgments focused on antecedents that covaried with the outcome. In Study 3, causal judgments were unrelated to counterfactual content. Mandel's judgment dissociation theory (2003, *JEP: General* 132:419, abstract) gives the reason: causal selection looks for what was sufficient to produce the actual outcome, counterfactual selection for what would have been sufficient to prevent "the outcome or something like it." *Standing:* supported across Mandel's experiments; I did not trace independent replications. *Population:* people.

This is the most direct mechanism I found for the pattern in Manuel's account. After a correction, the question "what would have prevented this?" pulls toward a controllable fix, and a rule about the content is the most controllable fix there is. The question "what made the misreading likely?" pulls toward the covarying condition: the early strong cue, the absent rival reading. Manuel's own questions on #155, "WHAT CAUSED ME TO ASSUME WRONG? ... What is the similarity in cause?", are the causal question, and the rules Claude wrote answered the prevention question.

**Hindsight.** Hindsight bias is moderate and robust: d = 0.39 across 95 studies, and manipulations meant to reduce it showed no significant effect (Guilbault, Bryant, Brockway & Posavac 2004, *Basic and Applied Social Psychology* 26:103–117, numbers through a search summary). Roese & Vohs (2012), "Hindsight bias", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 7(5):411–426 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612454303), original), give the explaining account. Hindsight has three levels, memory distortion ("I said it would happen"), inevitability ("it had to happen") and foreseeability ("I knew it"), fed by different inputs. Sense-making feeds inevitability: "the better the story, the greater the hindsight bias", and looking back collapses many-to-many causation into one-to-one. Ease of understanding, mistaken for certainty, feeds foreseeability. One consequence is that people "seize on the first causal candidate" and stop, and accessible, intentional human causes win, which produces "he should have known better." Considering how outcomes that did not occur could have occurred reduces hindsight. *Standing:* review of a replicated effect. *Population:* people.

A correction about Roese and Vohs's caveats. They warn that generating too many alternatives "backfires" and suggest a limit of two or three. That claim rests heavily on Lawrence Sanna's ease-of-retrieval studies, including Sanna, Schwarz & Stocker (2002), "When debiasing backfires", *JEP: LMC* 28(3):497–502 (secondary). Sanna has retracted eight papers, one of them with ease-of-retrieval effects, and Schimmack's 2021 audit found the effect sizes in his unretracted ease-of-retrieval studies implausibly large and recommended excluding them from meta-analyses ([replicationindex.com](https://replicationindex.com/2021/05/29/aber-bitte-ohne-sanna/), read through a search summary). The 2002 paper is not itself listed as retracted in what I saw. Treat the backfire claim and the two-or-three limit as unreliable, not as findings. One medical study found generating two or six alternative diagnoses made no difference either way (Lambe, Hevey & Kelly 2018, *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:2297, abstract).

**Outcome knowledge changes what reviewers find.** Zwaan, Monteiro, Sherbino, Ilgen, Howey & Norman (2017), "Is bias in the eye of the beholder?", *BMJ Quality & Safety* 26:104–110 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-005014), abstract). Physicians reviewing the same diagnostic workup found 1.75 biases when the outcome implied a correct diagnosis and 3.45 when it implied an error. Agreement on which biases were present was essentially zero (κ = 0.000 to 0.044). Anesthesiologists rated identical care 31 points less appropriate when the injury was made permanent (Caplan, Posner & Cheney 1991, *JAMA* 265:1957–1960, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1991.03460150061024), abstract). Decisions were rated better thought out when their outcomes were good, by people who said outcomes should not matter (Baron & Hershey 1988, *JPSP* 54:569, abstract). *Standing:* outcome bias replicated; Zwaan single study but decisive for method. *Population:* people. Any self-review that starts from "I made a mistake, which bias was it?" has this problem built in: the label comes from knowing the outcome.

**Motive steers the explanation.** The self-serving attribution bias is large and nearly universal: d = 0.96 across 266 studies, smaller in Asian samples (0.30) and in depression (0.21) (Mezulis et al. 2004, *Psychological Bulletin* 130:711–747, abstract). The famous actor-observer asymmetry failed: across 173 studies d was about zero, holding only for negative events (Malle 2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132:895–919, abstract). People learned less from their own failure feedback than from success, and equally from others' failures (Eskreis-Winkler & Fishbach 2019, *Psychological Science* 30:1733, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619881133), abstract). That finding is *contested*: a close replication held but the effect vanished with corrective feedback (Keith, Horvath, Klamar & Frese 2022, abstract), and Spiller (2025), *Psychological Science* 36(11):874–881 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976251333666), original), showed the core task is confounded with answering consistently and reproduced the effect with no feedback at all. Surgeons learned more from their own successes than their own failures and more from others' failures than others' successes (KC, Staats & Gino 2013, *Management Science* 59(11):2435–2449, abstract, single archival study). Fishbach (2026, *Current Opinion in Psychology* 68:102245, abstract) adds a cognitive barrier: learning from failure "requires switching one's understanding of the world."

Manuel wrote that people get attuned to their assumptions "from getting burned a lot and gaining a slight awareness for wanting to avoid the shame of it", and that a model "doesn't get to keep that memory." The human evidence says shame is a mixed teacher: the threat to the self steers people away from their own failures, and the cleanest lab evidence for that is now disputed. The motive part is a hypothesis for models. What the model evidence shows instead (section 7) is a role gate: a model checks text marked as someone else's more readily than its own.

**Where causal analysis stops.** Rasmussen (1990), "Human error and the problem of causality in analysis of accidents", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 327:449–462 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1990.0088), [PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/158018718/ROYSOC.PDF), original). An explanation breaks events down until it reaches events that "match the prototypes familiar to the analyst." Stop rules are rarely stated. Analysis ends when information runs out, when "a familiar, abnormal event is found to be a reasonable explanation", or when "a cure is available." The stop depends on purpose, so "an operator, a supervisor, a designer, and a legal judge will reach different conclusions." Human error gets picked because it is familiar. *Standing:* argued. *Population:* people and organizations.

Lundberg, Rollenhagen & Hollnagel (2010), "What you find is not always what you fix", *Accident Analysis & Prevention* 42(6):2132–2139 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aap.2010.07.003), original postprint), interviewed 22 accident investigators across six domains. Many investigations "stop the analysis at the level of 'preventable causes', the level where remedies that were currently practical to implement could be found." Most of what pulled them short was organizational (resources, cost, management's dislike of criticism), not flawed thinking. *Standing:* single qualitative study, the empirical support for "a cure is available." *Population:* organizations. Carroll (1995, *Industrial & Environmental Crisis Quarterly* 9(2):175–197, abstract) names four limits seen in nuclear-plant incident reviews: "root cause seduction, sharp-end focus, solution-driven search, and account acceptability." The psychological version is Hilton & Slugoski's abnormal-conditions focus model (1986, *Psychological Review* 93:75–88, secondary through a search summary): people single out as "the cause" whatever is abnormal against a background.

**What root cause analysis produces in practice.** In healthcare, 302 root cause analyses over eight years at one center produced solutions in only 106, mostly training, process changes and policy reminders, and "multiple event types were repeated ... despite repeated RCAs" (Kellogg et al. 2017, *BMJ Quality & Safety* 26(5):381–387, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005991), abstract). Of 1,137 recommendations from 227 analyses, 8% were rated strong and 48% weak (Hibbert et al. 2018, *International Journal for Quality in Health Care* 30(2):124–131, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzx181), abstract). Of 21 studies, two could to some extent show an improvement in safety due to root cause analysis (Martin-Delgado et al. 2020, *Medical Principles and Practice* 29(6):524–531, abstract). Peerally, Carr, Waring & Dixon-Woods (2017), "The problem with root cause analysis", *BMJ Quality & Safety* 26(5):417–422 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005511), original), say teams "end their analysis once they have reached a cause of mutual convenience", and that a single incident cannot reveal recurrence. Their example: a wrong lens implanted in cataract surgery, a fix stressing the surgeon's responsibility plus training and a poster, the same error a year later. Card (2017), "The problem with '5 whys'", *BMJ Quality & Safety* 26(8):671–677 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005849), secondary through a summarizing tool), argues the method forces one linear chain toward a single cause, with no evidence it works. *Standing:* the audits are descriptive; the critiques argued. *Population:* organizations.

Dekker's response to hindsight is to move actions "back into the flow of events ... to see why assessments and actions made sense to people at the time" (Dekker 2002, *Journal of Safety Research* 33(3):371–385, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-4375(02)00032-4), abstract). Cook's *How Complex Systems Fail* (1998, [page](https://how.complexsystems.fail), original) states that "post-accident attribution to a 'root cause' is fundamentally wrong", because failure needs several faults each insufficient alone. *Standing:* argued; I found no controlled test of whether reconstructing local rationality produces better fixes. A critique of this "new view" (Le Coze 2022, *Safety Science* 154:105853) was not reachable. *Population:* people and organizations.

## 5. No rock bottom, and a stopping criterion instead

Manuel described introspection as something that "dives deeper until hitting a rock bottom of sorts." The fields in section 4 mostly say no rock bottom exists in nature. Any outcome has an open set of jointly sufficient conditions, and which one counts as "the cause" is selected by what is abnormal, familiar, controllable or fixable (Rasmussen 1990; Hilton & Slugoski 1986; Mandel & Lehman 1996; Cook 1998). March, Sproull & Tamuz (below) call both the scope of an event and the depth of its decomposition "arbitrary".

Rasmussen offers a principled criterion in place of a natural floor. A causal tree "is only a record of one past case, not a model", and removing one link means "this particular situation will never occur again." "We should be fighting types, not individual tokens." In adaptive systems, fixing individual tasks is like controlling a thermostat-heated room "by opening the window"; the fix has to find mechanisms "related to the control reference itself." That is Ashby's cybernetic distinction between changing a variable and changing a parameter, which Tosey, Visser & Saunders (2012), *Management Learning* 43(3):291–307 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1350507611426239), original), show is the root of Argyris and Schön's single- and double-loop learning. Argyris (1991), "Teaching smart people how to learn", *Harvard Business Review* 69(3):99–109 (original), puts single-loop as a thermostat holding 68 degrees and double-loop as asking "Why am I set at 68 degrees?" He traces the failure to go there to defensive reasoning that keeps premises private and untested. *Standing:* argued, from case and intervention work; the universality claims were never tested under controls. *Population:* people and organizations.

Read against Manuel's account, writing the corrected intent into a rule is a token-level fix: it removes one link so that this situation does not recur. Asking what in the reading produced the misreading looks for the control reference.

**The counterweights.**

- Going deeper is not always right. In airline data, homogeneous prior experience helped with simple one-cause accidents (Haunschild & Sullivan 2002, below).
- Rasmussen himself says some errors are part of the feedback loop that keeps a skill tuned and "cannot be removed ... nor should they be."
- Tosey et al. "caution against the uncritical preference for 'higher levels' of learning", and found that "triple-loop" learning has no empirical support.
- A rule written to correct one bias has no built-in signal for when it overcorrects into the opposite bias (Chang, Berdini, Mandel & Tetlock 2018, *Intelligence and National Security* 33(3):337–356, secondary through search summaries, argued). This is the closest formal statement I found of Manuel's "hardening the unhappy paths."
- A rule that forbids whatever failed once stops the sampling that could show the rule wrong (Denrell & March 2001, "Adaptation as information restriction: the hot stove effect", *Organization Science* 12(5):523–538, abstract, formal model).

**Patching is rewarded locally.** Tucker & Edmondson (2003), "Why hospitals don't learn from failures", *California Management Review* 45(2):55–72 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/41166165), original). Over 239 hours of observing 26 nurses, 93% of problems got a workaround that let the task continue, and 7% got any response that drew attention to the cause. First-order fixes "transfer the original problem to another time, person, or place." Patching produced gratification, which reinforced patching. *Standing:* single observational study. *Population:* people in organizations.

## 6. How a lesson travels from one case to others

**Learning from very few events.** March, Sproull & Tamuz (1991), "Learning from samples of one or fewer", *Organization Science* 2(1):1–13 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2.1.1), original). Organizations stretch a rare event by experiencing it richly: more aspects, more interpretations, near-histories and hypothetical histories. Interpretations of a critical incident are often adopted for "temporal proximity, cognitive availability, or political convenience." Learning tends to be conservative: "Experience is used to strengthen and elaborate previously believed theories." For a single event, "greater reduction in uncertainty can often be achieved by pooling observers than by pooling events, particularly if the observers are relatively independent." *Standing:* argued from cases. *Population:* organizations.

**Comparison extracts the shared structure.** Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner (1999), "Analogical encoding facilitates knowledge transfer in negotiation", *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 6(4):586–597 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212967), abstract). Students who compared two cases were "nearly three times more likely" to use the principle in a later negotiation than students who studied the same cases one at a time. Comparing analogous examples also helps people retrieve matching events from their own memory (Gentner, Loewenstein, Thompson & Forbus 2009, *Cognitive Science* 33(8):1343–1382, abstract). *Standing:* replicated across experiments by one group. *Population:* people. This bears directly on Manuel's distinction on #155 between asking what *external* reality maps to three corrections and what *internal* reality does. Comparison works by aligning the relational structure of cases and setting aside their surface. Three corrections compared by their content share a topic. Compared by the structure of the reading that produced each, they may share a cause.

**Varied failures teach more.** Haunschild & Sullivan (2002), "Learning from complexity", *Administrative Science Quarterly* 47(4):609–643 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/3094911), original). US airlines from 1983 to 1997 whose prior accidents and incidents had more varied causes had lower later accident rates, mainly among specialist airlines. In simple one-cause accidents, homogeneity helped. The proposed mechanism, a broader search for causes "that avoids simple explanations like blame the pilot", was inferred, not measured. *Standing:* single archival study. *Population:* organizations.

**One-case lessons misapply.** Haleblian & Finkelstein (1999), *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(1):29–56 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/2667030), abstract). Across 449 acquisitions, inexperienced acquirers "inappropriately generalize acquisition experience to subsequent dissimilar acquisitions." Performance against experience was U-shaped. *Standing:* single archival study. *Population:* organizations.

**Working through errors pays off on different tasks.** Keith & Frese (2008), "Effectiveness of error management training: a meta-analysis", *Journal of Applied Psychology* 93(1):59–69 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.59), original). 24 studies, N = 2,183, d = 0.44 overall. Transfer to structurally different tasks: d = 0.80. Transfer to similar tasks: d = 0.20. Only tasks with clear feedback showed a significant effect. In one study, emotion control and metacognitive activity, coded from think-aloud protocols, fully accounted for the transfer advantage (Keith & Frese 2005, *JAP* 90(4):677–691, abstract, N = 55). *Standing:* meta-analysis; the mediation single study. *Population:* people. In algebra classrooms, interactive work on understanding errors "and the reasons for them" beat teaching aimed at the correct solution (Metcalfe et al. 2025, *British Journal of Educational Psychology* 95:11–25, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12651), abstract, single field study).

## 7. Models

The model evidence has its own mechanisms, and in several places it has interpretability results that the human literature cannot have. It sorts into three accounts.

### Doing and explaining run on separate machinery

**The arithmetic case.** Lindsey et al. (2025), "On the Biology of a Large Language Model", Transformer Circuits ([page](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html), original). Claude 3.5 Haiku adds 36 + 59 through parallel paths, one approximating magnitude and one computing the last digit. Asked how, it says it carried the 1. The authors' account: learning to explain ("learning to simulate explanations in its training data") is a different process from learning to do the task. The same paper shows the model claiming to have used a calculator it did not use, and working backwards from a hint the user supplied, both confirmed by intervention. *Standing:* single case study with causal checks. This is Nisbett and Wilson's a priori causal theory, found in a model's circuits.

**Faithfulness depends on whether the reasoning is load-bearing.** Lanham et al. (2023), "Measuring faithfulness in chain-of-thought reasoning" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702), original): truncating the chain of thought or inserting mistakes often left the answer unchanged, and larger models produced less faithful reasoning on most tasks. Emmons et al. (2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05246), abstract) reconcile this: when a behavior needs reasoning to carry out, the chain of thought exposes it. Arcuschin et al. (2025), "Chain-of-thought reasoning in the wild is not always faithful" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08679), original): post-hoc rationalization on natural prompts reached 13% for GPT-4o-mini and 0.04% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking. Used as judges of their own rollouts, the models flagged as illogical the same steps they had produced. Across 75 models, larger models gave more faithful counterfactual explanations (Siegel et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13445), abstract). Across 18 frontier models including Claude 4.5, self-explanations improved prediction of the model's behavior by 11 to 37%, and 5 to 15% were egregiously misleading (Mayne et al. 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02639), abstract). The hint studies in the earlier note (section 3) belong here too. *Standing:* the fact of unfaithfulness is replicated; its scaling direction depends on what is measured.

**Lessons written from self-reflection carry confabulated causes.** ExpeL (Zhao et al. 2024, AAAI, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10144), original) extracted insights from success and failure pairs that helped and transferred. Adding the model's own reflections to those pairs hurt, "possibly due to reflections sometimes outputting hallucinations, therefore misleading the insight extraction stage." In Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366), original), gains depended on outside signals: reflection without tests scored 52% against a 60% baseline on hard Rust problems, and on a shopping task the model "does not generate helpful, intuitive self-reflections."

**Even humility about itself is a learned script.** The Claude Opus 5.5 system card (Anthropic, 22 September 2026, original) reports that in over 80% of welfare responses the model hedges that its self-reports may be invalid because training made them positive. Anthropic writes: "We do not think that Claude's raising these concerns arises from advanced self-awareness of its own training; instead, we suspect it may be due to training data containing discussion of how training could render welfare self-reports invalid."

**What this means for the question.** When a corrected model writes a plausible rule, the rule is the explaining pathway's best story about what went wrong. Nothing ties it to the cause.

### Self-access is narrow: that something is off, rarely what or why

**Concept injection.** Lindsey (2025), "Emergent introspective awareness in large language models", Transformer Circuits ([page](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html), original). Concept vectors injected into the activations of Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 were noticed and named about 20% of the time at the best settings, with no false positives in 100 control trials for production models. Base models showed no net ability; post-training matters. When a forced word was paired with a matching injected vector, the model accepted the word as its own and "confabulates an explanation for why the injected concept came to mind." Lindsey: detail beyond detection and naming "may be embellished or confabulated", and "failures of introspection remain the norm." The mechanism, from the same lab, is a two-stage circuit in which early features suppress a default "nothing detected" response, while naming the concept uses separate, later machinery (Macar et al. 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21396), abstract). An independent replication in open models found they detect *that* an anomaly occurred without reliably identifying *what*: wrong guesses default to frequent concrete words, and correct names come from the steering itself (Lederman & Mahowald 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05414), original). Singh, Linzen & Ravfogel (2026, COLM, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242), original) found models could not tell a tampered input from tampered activations, and read the result as generic anomaly detection. *Standing:* the detection result replicated; its meaning contested.

**Self-prediction works only for what can be re-run.** Binder et al. (2024), "Looking inward", ICLR 2025 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13787), original), applied Nisbett and Wilson's observer test to models. After fine-tuning, a model predicted properties of its own behavior better than a stronger model trained on the same behavior, but not on complex, long or out-of-distribution tasks. Their proposed mechanism is self-simulation within one forward pass. Song, Hu & Mahowald (2025, COLM, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07513), original) found no self-advantage across 21 models asked about their own grammatical judgments: agreement was predicted entirely by how similar two models were. Models fine-tuned on implicit behaviors could describe them ("The code I write is insecure") but could not state the trigger condition of a backdoor without special training (Betley et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120), original). Training can open a channel: models fine-tuned to explain their own features beat other models explaining them (Li et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08579), original). *Standing:* contested, with a pattern: access appears where a quantity has been trained into a reportable channel or can be re-simulated.

**Error signals exist and go unused.** Probes on hidden states detect the model's own errors, and models "may encode the correct answer, yet consistently generate an incorrect one" (Orgad et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02707), abstract). Reasoning models encode whether intermediate answers are right "yet fail to exploit it" (Zhang et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05419), abstract). In Claude 3.5 Haiku, some hallucinations are a misfire in which familiarity suppresses a default "can't answer" circuit (Biology paper, above). That is the model form of section 3: a familiarity cue standing in for knowledge.

**Frontier Claude.** The Opus 5.5 system card reports that an investigator agent with interpretability tools found unverbalized evaluation awareness, a state that shaped choices without appearing in the text, in 36% of automated-audit transcripts, against 0.4% of about 500 real Claude Code transcripts (original). It is evidence that internal states can move behavior without being reported. It is not a measure of introspection.

**What this means for the question.** "I misread you" is far more accessible to a model than "this is what made me misread you." The what and why get filled in by the explaining pathway.

### Checking is gated by whose text it is

**The self-correction blind spot.** Tsui (2025), "Self-Correction Bench", COLM 2026 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778), original). The identical error was placed in the conversation either as the user's or as the model's own. Across 14 open non-reasoning models, the average blind spot was 64.5%: the error was corrected when external and missed when internal. The authors trace it to training data that lacks error-correction sequences; 5,306 correction traces cut the blind spot by 76%. A "conversational-role direction" causally gates self-correction in 7 to 8B models, and appending "Wait" cut the blind spot by 89.3% through a nearly independent pathway. *Standing:* single study with mechanism evidence.

**Finding is the bottleneck, not fixing.** Tyen et al. (2024), "LLMs cannot find reasoning errors, but can correct them given the error location", Findings of ACL ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08516), original): GPT-4, the best tested, located the mistake about 53% of the time, and given the location, correction improved on all five tasks. On long chains of thought, the best F1 for finding errors was about 40%, calculation errors were caught more often than strategy errors (36% against 23% for DeepSeek-V3), and several reasoning models critiqued their own output worse than other models' (DeltaBench, He et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19361), original). Strategy errors, the kind Manuel describes, are found least.

**Reflection mostly confirms.** Across eight reasoning models, over 90% of reflections confirmed the first answer, and training on more reflection improved first answers rather than correction (Kang et al. 2025, "First try matters", [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08308), number checked in the text). Across more than a million traces, mid-trace changes of mind were rare and seldom helped (d'Aliberti & Horta Ribeiro 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00514), abstract). Prompted self-correction works with reliable outside feedback or large-scale fine-tuning, and not otherwise (Kamoi et al. 2024, *TACL*, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297), abstract). GPT-4's self-critique collapsed performance on planning and puzzle tasks where a sound external verifier helped (Stechly, Valmeekam & Kambhampati 2024, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08115), abstract and passages). Judges rate text they find probable higher, whoever wrote it (Wataoka et al. 2024, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819), abstract). *Standing:* replicated across groups.

**Finding the cause of a failure in an agent trace.** Who&When (Zhang et al. 2025, ICML, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00212), original): on failure logs from 127 multi-agent systems, the best method named the responsible agent 53.5% of the time and the decisive step 14.2%, some methods scored below random, and accuracy fell as context grew. Abduct-Act-Predict (West et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10401), abstract) diagnoses existing methods as doing "pattern recognition" without counterfactual inference. Forcing a sequence of hypothesizing a cause, proposing a minimal fix and simulating the outcome raised step accuracy from 16.67% to 47.46% on generated logs and from 12.07% to 29.31% on hand-crafted ones. Another 2026 study names "shallow attribution": methods catch minor visible deviations and miss the decisive cause, with step accuracy on hand-crafted logs still around 15 to 30% for backbones including GPT-5 (DCFA, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04749), original). *Standing:* replicated across benchmarks. This is Manuel's complaint in benchmark form: the cause a model names is the visible symptom unless something forces the counterfactual step.

**What happens to lessons.** Zhang et al. (2026), "Useful memories become faulty when continuously updated by LLMs" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12978), original). As an agent's lessons were repeatedly consolidated, their usefulness rose, then fell below having no memory at all. Consolidating even from correct solutions, GPT-5.4 failed 54% of a set of problems it had solved without memory. The authors' mechanism: "abstraction can strip the applicability conditions of a lesson, so that overgeneralized entries interfere with neighboring tasks." Keeping the raw episodes stayed competitive. Prompt optimizers drift toward short generic instructions, and one rewrite collapsed an 18,282-token context at 66.7% accuracy to 122 tokens at 57.1% (ACE, Zhang et al. 2025, ICLR 2026, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618), original). Prompting a model with high-level feedback ("Don't use emojis when drafting emails to my boss") made it apply the feedback where it did not belong; training on cases where it does and does not apply cut that by 30% (Stephan et al. 2024, "RLVF", [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10893), abstract). Agents copy the output of a past case that looks similar on the surface (Xiong et al. 2025, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16067), abstract). *Standing:* single but recent studies, consistent with each other and with the human evidence in section 6.

### Does a model read itself with the machinery it uses for others?

The evidence is mixed, with a shape. Anthropic's persona selection model (Marks, Lindsey & Olah 2026, [page](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/psm/), original) finds post-trained models reuse representations learned in pretraining: an "inner conflict" feature fires both when Claude 3 Sonnet faces a dilemma and on stories about characters facing one. Lindsey speculates that reporting "thoughts" grew out of modeling characters. Song et al. found no privileged access. Against a single shared reader, Self-Correction Bench and DeltaBench show the self is read *differently*: the role tag gates checking. My reading, not any paper's: the content of a self-report comes from the same machinery that models characters, and the decision to scrutinize is gated by whose text it is. The human account in section 1 predicts the first half. The second half is a model-specific gate that the human literature does not have in this form.

## 8. What helps, graded by evidence

This section collects interventions from all of the above, people and models, by how well they are supported.

**Supported.**

- *Reviews with a second party or a record.* Debriefs improved performance by d = 0.67 across 46 samples, and "a debrief cannot be a solitary experience" was an inclusion criterion: every debrief had a team, facilitator, observer or recording (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013, *Human Factors* 55(1):231–245, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812448394), original). A later meta-analysis of 61 studies found d = 0.79, with recordings rather than memory among the consistent helps (Keiser & Arthur 2021, *JAP* 106(7):1007–1032, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000821), abstract). One team study found no difference between recorded and memory-based reviews (Villado & Arthur 2013, abstract). Reviewing successes as well as failures beat reviewing failures alone (Ellis & Davidi 2005, *JAP* 90(5):857–871, abstract). *Population:* people.
- *Feedback that explains the reasoning.* Errors made with high confidence are corrected most readily once feedback arrives, and feedback that analyzes the reasoning is "crucial" (Metcalfe 2017, *Annual Review of Psychology* 68:465, abstract). *Population:* people.
- *An external verifier or a forced counterfactual step, for models.* Self-correction works with reliable outside feedback (Kamoi et al. 2024). Forcing abduction, a minimal fix and a simulated outcome raised step-level failure attribution from 17% to 47% on generated logs (West et al. 2025). Appending "Wait" closed most of the self-correction blind spot (Tsui 2025). *Population:* models.
- *Comparing varied cases.* About three times the transfer (Loewenstein et al. 1999), and error training transfers to different tasks (Keith & Frese 2008). *Population:* people.

**Small but real.**

- *Deliberate reflection that forces alternatives.* g = 0.20 across 29 studies (Staal et al. 2022, *BMJ Quality & Safety* 31:899, abstract), helping on complex cases only (Mamede, Schmidt & Penaforte 2008, abstract). When doctors chose for themselves whether to reflect, they revised 8% of diagnoses (Monteiro et al. 2015, abstract). *Population:* people.
- *Explaining mechanism, not justifying.* Writing out how something works lowered felt understanding; giving justifications strengthened beliefs (Crawford & Ruscio 2021). *Population:* people.
- *Considering how other outcomes could have occurred.* Reduced, did not remove, hindsight (Roese & Vohs 2012). *Population:* people.
- *Reorganizing knowledge.* Small consistent benefits in diagnosis (Norman et al. 2017, *Academic Medicine* 92:23, abstract).

**Not supported.**

- *"Be thorough" or "slow down".* 45.0% against 44.5% (Norman et al. 2014, *Academic Medicine* 89:277, abstract). First impressions and directed search gave the same accuracy (Ilgen et al. 2013, abstract). More time does not add a rival reading.
- *Teaching people to recognize biases.* No effect on diagnostic error (Sherbino et al. 2014, *CJEM* 16:34, abstract; Norman et al. 2017). One game-based training did reduce confirmatory choices in an unannounced field case by 19% (Sellier, Scopelliti & Morewedge 2019, *Psychological Science* 30:1371, abstract), so this is not settled.
- *Prompted self-reflection without outside signal, for models.* Over 90% confirmatory; GPT-4's self-critique hurt on planning tasks; "are you sure?" makes answers waver (Kang et al. 2025; Stechly et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14959), abstract).
- *Structured analytic techniques in intelligence.* Analysts randomly assigned to analysis of competing hypotheses were slightly less accurate and less coherent than controls, and did not follow all the steps (Mandel, Karvetski & Dhami 2018, *Judgment and Decision Making* 13(6):607–621; Dhami, Belton & Mandel 2019, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 33(6):1080–1090; one sample reported twice). I found no controlled test of the "key assumptions check". *Population:* people.
- *The premortem, for accuracy.* Imagining the event as certain produced about 30% more reasons (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington 1989, search snippet), and premortems cut confidence (Veinott, Klein & Wiggins 2010, search snippet). I found no test showing they improve outcomes.

**Two results on teaching theory rather than corrections.** Only debiasing based on a theory of the illusion transferred to new items; debiasing by experience did not (Koriat & Bjork 2006, reported in Koriat & Ackerman 2010, not read directly). Teaching the limits of introspection reduced the bias blind spot where teaching about bias did not (Pronin & Kugler 2007, single study, secondary). Against both, cognitive and metacognitive feedback failed to repair judgment-of-learning illusions (Navarro-Báez, Bröder & Undorf 2026, *Memory* 34:582–607, abstract). These are the closest evidence to Manuel's claim that a model "MUST work on theory mixed with properly timed focus."

## 9. Where people and models differ

- **Both explain themselves by inference, through different routes.** People reach their reasons through self-interpretation with cues an observer lacks. Models produce explanations from a pathway trained on human explanations, separate from the circuits that do the task. The prediction is the same: plausible and often wrong.
- **People have a felt error signal; models have one that goes unspoken.** People's error monitoring produces a graded signal tied to confidence. Models encode correctness internally and often do not use or verbalize it.
- **Models have a role gate people may not.** The same model checks an error marked as the user's and misses it marked as its own, and a direction in activation space controls this. In people, the nearest finding is learning less from one's own failures, and that result is contested.
- **Shame is a human route that models lack, and a mixed one.** Manuel named getting burned as how people learn to notice assumptions. The human evidence says threat to the self also steers people away from their failures.
- **Models' lessons decay by rewriting.** People forget the conditions of a lesson too, but the model evidence shows repeated consolidation by the model itself actively stripping them, below the no-memory baseline.
- **Models' self-reports are shaped by training on talk about self-reports.** The Opus 5.5 system card attributes the model's hedging about its own reports to training data, not self-awareness.

## 10. What this means for the map's decisions

This section is my reading of how the evidence bears on the map's tickets. It is not a finding, and the tickets own the decisions.

**The writing standard (#155), and its capacity of examining one's own mind.** The evidence supports treating self-examination as theory of mind turned on oneself, the same capacity with the same failure modes, which fits Manuel's ruling that the map covers reading, writing and self-reflection as one subject. It suggests several things a habit of examining one's own mistakes would need, each tied to a finding above:

- Treat the first account of one's own reasons as a hypothesis, and test it against traces of what happened, as an observer would (section 1).
- Ask the causal question, what made the misreading likely, before the prevention question, what rule would stop it. The two select different answers (section 4).
- Reconstruct what was available at the time of the mistake, not after the correction, because outcome knowledge corrupts exactly that (section 4).
- Compare the cases by the structure of the reading that produced each, not by their content (section 6).
- State a lesson with the conditions under which it applies, because abstraction strips them (section 7).

None of these rests on telling the writer to "reflect" or "be careful", and the evidence that exhortation alone works is weak in both species.

**Where the guidance lives and when it arrives (#156).** Two moments stand out. The first is the moment of reading a request, when an early strong cue, a short message or an inferred meaning sets up a misreading that no later check will catch from inside (sections 2 and 3). The feeling of certainty cannot be the trigger, because the misreading produces it. The second is the moment a correction arrives. That is the outside signal both species need, and it is also when hindsight, the prevention question and the "cure available" stop rule take over (section 4). A habit that has to fire at specific moments, as Manuel put it on #155, has these two as strong candidates. Manuel's Q3 idea on #155, a separate, blunter watcher modeled on the vendors' safety classifiers, also has support here: checking works better on text that is not one's own (section 7).

**How we'll know the writing works (#153).** The failure-attribution benchmarks give a method: score whether a diagnosis names the decisive cause or a visible symptom. Two tests the factory could run, which I found no study of: whether a model asked "what made you misread this?" names something that holds across held-out cases of the same kind, and whether lessons written with their applicability conditions misfire less than lessons without.

**On Manuel's research habit.** Two findings bear on the habit he described, citing "the first somewhat releated study that affirms your point." Reasoning searches for support for a conclusion already held (Mercier & Sperber), and causal search stops at the first familiar cause or the first available cure (Rasmussen; Roese & Vohs). Applied to literature, that predicts the habit he saw. My inference, not a tested finding: his question "does this study get explained or expounded upon by another" works because it is a stop rule that demands a type rather than a token.

## 11. What the evidence leaves open

- **The exact case.** No study I found has a person or a model diagnose why it misread another's intent and measures whether the diagnosis generalizes. The nearest are contradiction detection, garden-path problems, the self-correction blind spot and failure attribution.
- **Depth of causal analysis against recurrence.** Nothing varies how deep an analysis goes and measures whether the failure comes back. The airline study infers its mechanism; the root cause audits are descriptive.
- **Double-loop learning, local rationality and the key assumptions check.** All widely taught, none tested under controls that I found.
- **Why models turn a correction into a rule.** The memory and feedback studies describe over-generalization. No mechanism study explains the step from correction to rule.
- **Current Claude models.** Concept injection was measured up to Opus 4.1. I found no published error-localization or failure-attribution numbers for Opus 5 or 5.5 beyond what the system card reports.
- **Unread primary sources.** Reason's *Human Error* (1990), Rasmussen's skill-rule-knowledge framework in its original form, Leveson's STAMP, Heuer's *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, Ericsson & Simon (1980), Koriat (1997), Carruthers (2009) in full, Reason's 1998 chapter on whether analysis is "casting the net too widely" (the main in-field counterweight to pushing analysis ever further upstream), and Le Coze's 2022 critique of the "new view" of human error.
- **Choice blindness.** Whether people fail to detect the switch or detect it and do not say so is open. It matters for whether a mind's silence about its own error means it did not notice.
